## AI Summary

The European Commission's 2025 trade defence report records 32 new investigations, 26 definitive measures imposed and 232 measures in force at year end, 220 of them definitive. Provisional anti-dumping duties went on in 27 cases. In three cases the Commission applied measures retroactively, backdated to before the provisional stage. The report is aggregate: it names no product and no rate. The cost to an importer is therefore a risk to audit, not a rate to price, and the action is a product-line review plus a duty-risk clause in new contracts.

## What happened

The Commission adopted its 2025 Annual Trade Defence Report, and the news release accompanying it was published on 18 September 2026. The release gives the year in six numbers.

It opened **32 new trade-defence investigations** in 2025. That is just slightly below the record high set in 2024, so the pipeline of new cases did not fall away after the peak year.

It imposed **26 definitive trade defence measures** during 2025. A definitive measure is the end state of a case: the duty that stays in force for its full term rather than the provisional duty that holds the position while an investigation finishes.

By the end of 2025 the Commission had **232 trade defence measures in place**, and **220 of those were definitive**. The release states that these measures protect around 637,000 manufacturing jobs in the European Union.

Provisional duties were imposed in **27 anti-dumping cases** in 2025. That is the stage that matters most to an importer with cargo on the water, because a provisional duty starts the clock on money owed at the border.

And in **three cases** the Commission applied measures retroactively, with the measures backdated to before the introduction of provisional measures.

> **Retroactive duty** — a duty whose legal effect starts before the date it was imposed. In EU trade defence the ordinary sequence is an investigation, then a provisional duty, then a definitive duty. A retroactive measure reaches back past the provisional stage, so goods that entered before the provisional duty existed become liable for the duty as well. The importer's declaration date stops being the line that separates duty-free cargo from dutiable cargo.

The release is a summary, not a schedule. It does not name the three cases, the products, the countries of origin, the rates or the backdated periods. It does not name the 27 anti-dumping cases either. What it gives is the shape of the year: a heavy investigation load, a heavy definitive output, and a small but non-zero number of cases where the duty reached backwards.

## Why now

The report covers calendar 2025 and was released on 18 September 2026, so the year it describes is closed and the numbers are final rather than running totals. The 32 investigations are the ones that will produce provisional and definitive measures through 2026 and 2027. The 26 definitive measures are already at the border. The three retroactive cases are already settled, and any exposure they created has already been incurred by whoever held the goods.

## So what

The judgement is short. This report does not let an importer calculate a per-unit duty, because it carries no product, no origin, no rate and no period. What it does is tell an importer that the population of duty-bearing lines is large and stable, that new lines keep being added at close to a record pace, and that a small number of cases each year reach backwards past the provisional stage.

That last point is the one that changes behaviour. A provisional duty is visible. It is published, it has a start date, and an importer who reads the Official Journal knows when the border cost begins. A retroactive measure removes that certainty after the fact. Cargo that cleared at a zero rate becomes cargo that owed duty, and the bill arrives after the goods have been sold.

Work the arithmetic on the exposure rather than the rate. Take an importer with an annual import programme of EUR 4,000,000 in CIF value across a set of product lines. The report gives no rate, so the exercise is to ask what a duty would cost at each level rather than to assert one.

| Duty applied to the EUR 4,000,000 programme | Annual cost if the whole programme is in scope |
| --- | --- |
| 5% | EUR 200,000 |
| 10% | EUR 400,000 |
| 20% | EUR 800,000 |
| 30% | EUR 1,200,000 |

Those are arithmetic, not forecasts. The point of the table is the ratio: a duty in the range the EU routinely sets turns a thin import margin into a loss on the affected lines, and the affected lines are the ones an importer has not checked.

Now narrow it to the retroactive question. Suppose 10% of that programme, EUR 400,000 of CIF value, sits in a product line that later becomes one of the three retroactive cases. At a 20% duty the backdated bill is EUR 80,000. At 30% it is EUR 120,000. That is the amount that arrives with no warning, on goods already cleared and already sold, and it is the amount a duty-risk clause is written to move to the other side of the contract.

The second worked case is the in-transit one. An importer with a EUR 250,000 consignment on the water when a provisional duty is published faces the duty from the date the measure takes effect, and the report gives no date. If that consignment is caught at 20%, the cost is EUR 50,000 against a margin that was calculated without it. The operational answer is not to predict the date but to know which of your lines are in an open case, so the consignment is either accelerated, diverted or priced with the duty inside it.

The third case is the standing stock. Of the 232 measures in place, 220 are definitive. A definitive measure runs for its full term, so a product line that has been dutiable for years stays dutiable. An importer who has bought the same line from the same origin for three years without checking the measure list is carrying a cost that is already in the price and is not visible on the invoice as a separate item. Auditing the line turns a hidden cost into a negotiable one.

## FAQ

**Does the 2025 report tell me which products face retroactive duties?**

No. The news release gives the count — three cases — and nothing else about them. It does not name the products, the countries of origin, the duty rates or the periods the measures were backdated to. The same is true of the 27 anti-dumping cases in which provisional duties were imposed: the release gives the number and no case list. An importer who needs the named cases has to work from the Official Journal and the Commission's own case register, which sit outside this document. Treat the report as a signal about the volume of activity, not as a schedule of what is dutiable.

**How does a retroactive duty change what I owe at the border?**

A provisional duty has a start date, and goods declared before that date are outside it. A retroactive measure reaches back past the provisional stage, so goods that entered before the provisional duty existed become liable as well. The declaration date stops being the line that separates duty-free cargo from dutiable cargo. For an importer, the practical effect is that a consignment cleared at a zero rate can later carry a bill, and the bill arrives after the goods have been sold. That is why the exposure is a contract question as much as a customs question.

**Should I stop importing lines that sit in an open case?**

Not necessarily. An open investigation is not a duty, and many cases close without a definitive measure. The decision is about pricing and contract terms rather than about stopping. If a line is in an open case, price the consignment with a duty inside it, or write a clause that puts the duty on the supplier if it is imposed after the contract date. The 232 measures in force at the end of 2025, 220 of them definitive, show that a large standing population of dutiable lines is normal. The task is to know which of your lines are in that population.

## The point

The report is a count, not a rate. It tells an importer that the EU opened 32 investigations, imposed 26 definitive measures and held 232 measures in force at the end of 2025, and that three cases reached backwards past the provisional stage. None of that converts into a per-unit cost, because the document names no product and no rate. What it converts into is a decision: audit your product lines against the measures already in force and the cases already open, and put a duty-risk clause in every new contract so that a duty imposed after the signature, including a backdated one, lands on the side of the deal that agreed to carry it.
