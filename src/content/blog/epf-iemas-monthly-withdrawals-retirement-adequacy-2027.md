## What happened

Budget 2027 introduces a new way for Employees Provident Fund (EPF) members to take their retirement money. Prime Minister Datuk Seri Anwar Ibrahim announced that members aged 55 and 60 can opt for monthly withdrawals under the i-Emas scheme, allowing savings retained with the fund to continue earning annual dividends. Malay Mail reported the announcement on 5 November 2026.

The change responds to a specific problem. Lee Heng Guie, an economist quoted in the same report, said a survey had found that about 97 per cent of Malaysians withdrew their EPF savings in a lump sum at age 55 or 60, with many depleting the money within three to five years.

> **i-Emas:** a new EPF withdrawal option announced under Budget 2027 that lets members aged 55 and 60 take their savings as a monthly income stream rather than a single lump sum. The money left in the fund keeps earning annual dividends, so the account does not stop growing the day the member retires.

The two options work differently. A lump sum puts the entire balance in the member's hands at once. A monthly withdrawal pays out a set amount each month while the remainder stays invested with EPF and keeps earning dividends.

## Why now

The problem i-Emas addresses has been visible for years. The survey figure of 97 per cent taking lump sums, and the three-to-five-year depletion window, describe a pattern that predates this budget. What changed this month is the policy response.

The thread runs through the last two to three years of retirement policy. EPF has gradually added flexibility to how members access their savings, and the government has pushed schemes that keep money inside the fund longer. i-Emas fits that pattern: it is a mechanism that converts a one-time payout into a stream, and it keeps the balance inside the fund where it earns dividends.

The timing also tracks the budget cycle. Budget 2027 is the vehicle for new policy announcements, and retirement adequacy is a standing concern in Malaysian public finance. The i-Emas announcement is the government's answer to a question that has been asked repeatedly: what happens to retirees who take the lump sum and spend it?

## So what

The gain lands on retirees who struggle to pace their spending. A monthly withdrawal imposes a structure that a lump sum does not. The mechanism is simple: when the money arrives in small amounts over time, the retiree spends it in small amounts over time, and the balance that stays with EPF keeps earning dividends. That is the discipline argument for i-Emas.

The cost lands on retirees who need the lump sum for a specific purpose. A medical bill, a home repair, a child's wedding or a business start-up requires a large amount at once. A monthly stream does not provide that. The trade-off is real: i-Emas protects against running out of money, but it also locks money away from emergencies.

The third actor is the retiree who would have spent the lump sum wisely. For that person, i-Emas is a constraint without a benefit. The scheme assumes the problem is pacing, but the survey does not say how many of the 97 per cent depleted their savings because they spent carelessly and how many because they had unavoidable large expenses. That distinction is not in the report.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on Malaysian workers approaching retirement, and on the households they support. A worker who turns 55 or 60 after i-Emas takes effect faces a choice the previous cohort did not have: take everything at once, or take a monthly amount and let the rest keep earning dividends.

The concrete carrier is the retirement decision itself. A worker who chooses i-Emas trades immediate access for a longer income stream. That decision changes the household budget: monthly income arrives on a schedule, the EPF balance keeps growing, but a large unexpected expense cannot be met from the fund without breaking the arrangement.

The second carrier is the family that depends on the retiree's savings. The survey finding that many retirees deplete their lump sum within three to five years describes a household that runs out of money in its first half-decade of retirement. i-Emas is designed to stretch that money further, but it only works if the monthly amount covers the retiree's actual costs.

The third carrier is the broader retirement system. If i-Emas keeps more money inside EPF for longer, the fund holds a larger balance and pays dividends on it for more years. That is a structural change to how retirement savings flow, and it points a direction for future policy: expect more schemes that keep money inside the fund rather than releasing it.

## FAQ

**What is the i-Emas scheme introduced in Budget 2027?**

The i-Emas scheme is a new EPF withdrawal option announced under Budget 2027. It lets members aged 55 and 60 choose monthly withdrawals instead of taking a lump sum. The money retained with the fund continues to earn annual dividends, so the account does not stop growing at retirement. This differs from a lump sum withdrawal, where the entire balance is paid at once and the account is closed with no further dividends. The scheme is designed to provide a monthly income stream, trading immediate access for a longer payout period.

**Why was the i-Emas scheme introduced?**

The scheme responds to a survey finding cited by economist Lee Heng Guie. The survey found that about 97 per cent of Malaysians withdrew their EPF savings in a lump sum at age 55 or 60, with many depleting the money within three to five years. The government introduced i-Emas to address this pattern by offering a monthly withdrawal option that keeps the balance inside the fund earning dividends. The goal is to stretch retirement savings further and prevent retirees from running out of money in their first half-decade of retirement.

**What are the main differences between a lump sum and i-Emas monthly withdrawal?**

A lump sum withdrawal pays the entire balance at once, closes the account, and stops further dividends. An i-Emas monthly withdrawal pays a set amount each month while the remainder stays with EPF and keeps earning annual dividends. The lump sum gives immediate access to all money, useful for large expenses like medical bills or home repairs. The monthly option imposes spending structure, reducing the risk of depletion, but it locks money away from emergencies. The choice involves a trade-off between flexibility and discipline.

**Who does the i-Emas scheme affect?**

The scheme affects Malaysian workers approaching retirement, specifically those turning 55 or 60 after the policy takes effect. They face a new choice: take everything at once or take a monthly amount and let the rest earn dividends. It also affects households that depend on the retiree's savings, as a monthly stream changes the household budget. Additionally, it impacts the broader retirement system by keeping more money inside EPF for longer, which changes how retirement savings flow and points toward future policies that retain funds.

**What is a key limitation of the i-Emas scheme?**

A key limitation is that it assumes the problem is pacing, but the survey does not distinguish between retirees who depleted savings through careless spending and those who had unavoidable large expenses. For someone needing a lump sum for a medical bill, home repair, or business start-up, a monthly stream does not provide that. The scheme only helps if the monthly amount covers actual costs. If depletion was due to essential expenses, a monthly stream can only delay depletion, not prevent it.
