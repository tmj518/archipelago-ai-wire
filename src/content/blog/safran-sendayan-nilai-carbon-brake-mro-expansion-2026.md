## What happened

Safran Landing Systems, the landing gear and braking arm of the French aerospace group Safran, announced two investments in Negeri Sembilan on 28 October 2026, according to a media release from the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA), the federal agency that approves and promotes foreign manufacturing investment in Malaysia.

The first is a €134 million expansion of its existing plant at Sendayan. The money adds 7,000 square metres of advanced manufacturing space, including a new textile line, carbonisation furnaces and machining equipment. Carbonisation is the heating step that turns a woven fibre mat into carbon; the furnaces are the ovens that do it. The site currently employs 160 people and will double its carbon brake disc production capacity by 2028.

The second is a €2 million maintenance, repair and overhaul centre in Nilai with 1,000 square metres of floor space. It sits close to Kuala Lumpur International Airport and 20 kilometres from Sendayan, which supplies it with new and refurbished carbon brake discs.

To support the ramp-up, Safran plans to raise its Malaysian workforce to 220 employees by 2030 across its industrial and repair operations.

> **MRO:** maintenance, repair and overhaul — the business of taking a worn aircraft part, restoring it to an airworthy standard and returning it to service, rather than making a new one. An MRO centre earns its money on labour hours, tooling and certification, not on raw material.

## Why now

The two announcements are one decision, not two. A carbon brake disc wears down in service and has to be refurbished; the factory that makes the disc and the shop that restores it are the same business, separated by 20 kilometres of the North–South Expressway. Building the repair centre next to the plant shortens the loop between making a part and re-certifying it.

The thread runs two to three years. Safran has operated at Sendayan long enough to have cut its greenhouse gas emissions there by 60% between 2018 and 2025, using low-carbon local energy and by recovering process gases. That is a plant with a track record, not a greenfield bet. Doubling disc production capacity by 2028 is the next step on a curve the site is already on.

What surfaced this month is the pairing. A factory expansion alone is a capacity story. A factory plus a repair shop plus a stated headcount target is a skills story, and it is the first time the Malaysian footprint has been described as an industrial and MRO system rather than a single plant.

## So what

The gain lands on Safran, on the airlines that buy its brakes, and on the workers who learn the repair trade. The mechanism is vertical integration inside one small geography: the Nilai centre takes discs from Sendayan, refurbishes them, and returns them to service, which keeps the margin on the repair inside the group instead of sending worn discs to a third-party shop elsewhere in the region.

The judgement about the world is that aerospace repair work is moving toward the factory that made the part. A carbon brake heat sink — the stack of discs that absorbs the energy of a landing — is a specialised component, and refurbishing it requires the same furnaces, the same tooling and much of the same knowledge as making it. Co-locating the two is cheaper than shipping the part across an ocean twice.

Who pays is the airline, and through the airline the passenger. A repair done closer to the operating region cuts transport time and turnaround, which is what an airline with a fleet based in Asia is buying. The report gives no price for a refurbishment, so the size of that saving is not in the document.

The third actor is the worker. The 220 target is 60 more people than the 160 at Sendayan today, spread across two sites and reached by 2030. That is a slow ramp, not a hiring surge, and it tells a jobseeker that the openings will arrive in tranches as capacity comes on stream.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on Negeri Sembilan's aerospace corridor and on the specific skills the two plants need. The corridor already runs from Sendayan toward the airport, and Nilai now sits inside it as a repair node rather than a dormitory town on the way to KLIA.

The concrete carrier is the job. A carbon brake heat sink refurbishment technician works on the stack of discs that a landing heats to hundreds of degrees, inspecting for wear, measuring thickness and deciding whether a disc is restored or scrapped. That is a bench trade with a certification attached, closer to precision machining than to assembly-line work. The Sendayan expansion names a textile line, carbonisation furnaces and machining equipment, which points to process operators, furnace technicians and machinists alongside the repair bench.

The second carrier is the training pipeline. A worker who wants one of these jobs needs a mechanical or electrical engineering background, or a vocational qualification in machining or aircraft maintenance, and then type-specific training that only the employer can give. Polytechnics, community colleges and the UniKL network in the Klang Valley feed this corridor; a student choosing a course now is choosing for a plant that reaches full headcount in 2030.

The third carrier is the supplier. A 7,000 square metre expansion and a new repair shop both need tooling, calibration, logistics and facilities services, and some of that is bought locally. Small firms in Nilai and Seremban that serve the plant have a reason to look at what the expansion needs.

At Sendayan, a €134 million expansion adds 7,000 square metres of manufacturing space, including a new textile line, carbonisation furnaces and machining equipment, and doubles carbon brake disc production capacity by 2028. At Nilai, a €2 million maintenance, repair and overhaul centre of 1,000 square metres sits 20 kilometres from Sendayan and refurbishes the discs the plant makes. The two together form an industrial and repair system rather than a single factory.

**What skills will the new jobs need?**

The release names the equipment, and the equipment implies the trades. A textile line and carbonisation furnaces point to process operators and furnace technicians who run and monitor high-temperature cycles. The machining equipment points to machinists who cut and finish components to tight tolerances. The Nilai repair centre points to heat sink refurbishment technicians, who inspect and measure worn carbon discs and decide whether each is restored or scrapped. All of these are bench and process trades with certification attached, and the employer supplies the type-specific training on top of a mechanical, electrical or vocational base.

**How many jobs is this, and when do they arrive?**

The release gives one headcount figure: 220 employees across Malaysian industrial and MRO operations by 2030, against 160 at Sendayan as of 28 October 2026. That is a gap of 60 people. The document does not split the 60 between the two sites, does not say how many the Nilai centre employs when it opens, and does not give a start date for Nilai operations. The capacity doubling is dated 2028 and the workforce target 2030, so a jobseeker should expect hiring in stages across that window rather than a single intake.
