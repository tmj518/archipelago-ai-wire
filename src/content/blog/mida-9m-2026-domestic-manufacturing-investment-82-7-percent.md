## What happened

The Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA) published a media release on 17 November 2026 stating that domestic investment in manufacturing grew 82.7% year-on-year to RM37.8 billion in the first nine months of 2026. Domestic means the money comes from Malaysian sources rather than from a foreign investor.

The same release states that total domestic approved investment came to RM138.1 billion, a decline of 1.0% from the same period in 2025. So the manufacturing number rose sharply while the overall domestic number fell slightly. The two facts sit in the same document and both are true: the growth is concentrated in one sector, and it is being offset elsewhere in the domestic total.

MIDA also reported that the primary sector — the part of the economy that extracts raw material rather than processing it, such as plantations, mining and agriculture — recorded domestic investment growth of 345.2% to RM13.9 billion over the same nine months.

Across all sources, foreign and domestic, the release counts 4,387 approved projects, expected to create 152,090 jobs when fully implemented. Manufacturing as a whole, foreign plus domestic, drew RM106.3 billion.

> **Approved investment:** a project that has received MIDA's approval to proceed under Malaysia's investment promotion rules. It is a permission and an intention, not money already spent. The factory still has to be built, the machines installed and the workers hired. MIDA counts the jobs a project is expected to create "when fully implemented", which is a future state, not a current payroll.

## Why now

The nine-month reporting cycle is the immediate reason the number surfaced on 17 November. MIDA publishes approved-investment figures in batches, and the January–September cut is the last full picture before the year-end. A figure that covers three quarters is the point at which a trend becomes visible rather than a single month's noise.

The second thread is the split between foreign and domestic money. For several years the public conversation about Malaysian investment has been dominated by foreign projects — data centres, semiconductor plants, regional headquarters. The 17 November release puts a domestic number on the record at a scale that invites comparison: RM37.8 billion of Malaysian-owned manufacturing approvals against RM106.3 billion of total manufacturing approvals.

The third thread is the primary sector. A 345.2% rise to RM13.9 billion is a large proportional move, and it sits alongside the manufacturing rise in the same document. Read together, the release describes Malaysian capital moving into both processing and extraction in the same nine months.

## So what

The gain lands on the states and industrial parks that host the approved projects, and on the construction firms, machine suppliers and logistics operators that serve a factory before it opens. An approved manufacturing project is a building site first and a payroll second. The mechanism runs in that order: approval, then construction, then equipment installation, then hiring.

The cost lands on the domestic total. Total domestic approved investment fell 1.0% even as manufacturing rose 82.7%. That means other parts of the domestic total — services, or the parts of the economy outside manufacturing and primary — did not keep pace. A reader should not read the manufacturing number as a statement about the whole economy.

The third actor is the worker deciding where to look for a job. Approved investment is the earliest public signal of hiring, arriving months before a vacancy is posted. It is also the least reliable, because an approved project can be delayed, scaled down or never built. The release gives the size of the pipeline, not a completion schedule.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on the manufacturing workforce and on school leavers and graduates deciding what to train for. The concrete carrier is the factory job: a production operator, a technician, a quality inspector, a maintenance fitter. When RM37.8 billion of Malaysian-owned manufacturing projects are approved in nine months, the demand those projects generate is for people who can run a line, not only for the capital that funds it.

The second carrier is the state. Approved projects are sited somewhere, and the siting decision determines which local economy gets the construction work and later the payroll. MIDA's release gives the national total; the state-by-state split is a separate question, and readers following where investment lands can start with [the Johor data-centre story](/blog/mida-approved-investments-9m-2026-johor-data-centres/), which covers the same nine-month release from the state angle.

The third carrier is the supplier. A Malaysian-owned manufacturer buying machines, components and services tends to buy them closer to home than a foreign investor importing a turnkey line. That is an inference about behaviour, not a statement in the release, and it is the reason a domestic manufacturing number matters to small firms that will never appear in MIDA's project count.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** MIDA's media release of 17 November 2026 states the 82.7% growth to RM37.8 billion, the 345.2% growth to RM13.9 billion, the RM138.1 billion domestic total and its 1.0% decline, the RM106.3 billion manufacturing total, and the 152,090 expected jobs across 4,387 projects. Every figure in this article traces to that single document, retrieved on 17 November 2026. The release does not break the domestic manufacturing total down by state or by sub-sector in the passages used here.

**Inferred.** The reading that Malaysian-owned manufacturers are now the driver of approved investment growth is an inference from the gap between the manufacturing rate and the total domestic rate. The release states both numbers; it does not itself say domestic manufacturers are "driving" the total. The further inference that hiring follows approval with a lag of months is a mechanism, not a figure in the document, and the 152,090 jobs figure is MIDA's expectation at full implementation, not a count of positions filled.

**What would change my mind.** A state-level or sub-sector breakdown showing the RM37.8 billion concentrated in one or two very large projects would change the picture from a broad domestic manufacturing shift to a handful of approvals. A later MIDA release showing the same projects cancelled, delayed or revised downward would undercut the hiring signal. And a revised domestic total that reverses the 1.0% decline would change the "growth inside a flat total" framing entirely.

## How we calculated this

The release gives the domestic manufacturing total and its growth rate, and the total domestic figure and its change. The arithmetic below checks that the two statements are consistent with each other and shows how much of the domestic total manufacturing represents.

```text
Domestic manufacturing approved investment, Jan–Sep 2026: RM37.8 billion
Growth rate stated by MIDA: 82.7%
Implied Jan–Sep 2025 base: 37.8 / (1 + 0.827) = RM20.7 billion (rounded)
Check: 37.8 - 20.7 = RM17.1 billion increase, which is 17.1 / 20.7 = 82.6% (rounding)

Total domestic approved investment, Jan–Sep 2026: RM138.1 billion
Change stated by MIDA: -1.0%
Implied Jan–Sep 2025 total: 138.1 / (1 - 0.010) = RM139.5 billion (rounded)

Manufacturing share of the domestic total: 37.8 / 138.1 = 27.4%
Primary-sector share of the domestic total: 13.9 / 138.1 = 10.1%

Manufacturing approved investment, foreign plus domestic: RM106.3 billion
Domestic manufacturing as a share of that: 37.8 / 106.3 = 35.6%
```

The base-year figures and the shares are calculated here from MIDA's stated totals and rates. They are not printed in the release, and rounding means the reconstructed 2025 base is approximate.

## FAQ

**Does this mean RM37.8 billion is being spent in Malaysia this year?**
No. Approved investment is a permission to proceed under Malaysia's investment promotion rules, not money already committed to a building. The project still has to be financed, sited, built and equipped. MIDA counts the jobs a project is expected to create when fully implemented, which is a future state. The release gives the size of the pipeline; it does not give a spending schedule, a ground-breaking date or a completion date for any individual project. A reader should treat the figure as a signal of intent by Malaysian-owned manufacturers, and watch for construction activity and hiring notices as the later evidence that the intent became a factory.

**Why did total domestic investment fall if manufacturing rose so sharply?**
The two numbers measure different things. Manufacturing domestic investment rose 82.7% to RM37.8 billion. Total domestic approved investment, which includes services and other sectors alongside manufacturing and the primary sector, fell 1.0% to RM138.1 billion. A sharp rise in one component can sit inside a small decline in the total when the other components are larger or moving the other way. The release states both figures; it does not, in the passages used here, break the domestic total down far enough to show which sector produced the offset. That breakdown is the next document to look for.

**Which states and sectors will hire first?**
The release used here gives national totals and the manufacturing and primary-sector split. It does not give a state-by-state allocation of the RM37.8 billion, so any claim about which state hires first goes beyond this document. The mechanism a worker can rely on is the sequence: approval, then construction, then equipment installation, then hiring. Construction and site work come first and are often contracted locally; production hiring follows once a line is commissioned. Readers who want the state angle on the same nine-month release can follow [the Johor data-centre piece](/blog/mida-approved-investments-9m-2026-johor-data-centres/), and readers tracking industrial job openings in the north can read [the Ferrotec Kulim plant story](/blog/ferrotec-second-kulim-plant-semiconductor-jobs/).

## The point

The number that matters is not the 82.7%. It is the gap between 82.7% in manufacturing and a 1.0% decline in the domestic total. That gap says Malaysian capital moved into factory projects in the first nine months of 2026 while the rest of the domestic total did not grow. For a worker, that is a directional signal about where the next round of factory hiring is likely to come from, and it is a signal only — the release counts approvals and expected jobs, not buildings, machines or payslips.
