<h1>CS Allen and Indie Hackers: One-Person Businesses, Documented by Their Founders</h1>

<p>Indie Hackers is not a product you can copy in a weekend, but its core mechanic is. CS Allen built it around a single asset: an archive of interviews where founders of small, often one-person businesses publish their own revenue, pricing and growth numbers. The interview collection lives at <a href="https://www.indiehackers.com/interviews">indiehackers.com/interviews</a>, and each entry carries the founder's own figures rather than a reporter's estimate. This article examines how that archive works, how it was grown, what it earns, and which parts an individual can replicate without inventing a single number.</p>

<h2>Who CS Allen is and what Indie Hackers is</h2>

<p>CS Allen (Courtland Allen) is a software developer and writer based in the United States who created Indie Hackers. Before the project, his public footprint was as a developer and blogger; the Indie Hackers concept grew out of his interest in how small software businesses actually make money, and he built the site's early interviews himself. Indie Hackers is best described as a community plus an interview archive: a forum and a growing catalogue of structured conversations with the people behind independent, bootstrapped and often single-founder companies. The interviews ask the same questions repeatedly—what are you building, how do you get customers, how much revenue, what does it cost—which is what makes the archive comparable across entries. The interviews index is the primary source for everything below; each interview carries the founder's own numbers, so figures belong to the interviewee, not to Indie Hackers itself.</p>

<h2>The product, stated precisely</h2>

<p>The product is not one tool. It is a structured format for publishing business data plus a place for the people behind that data to talk to each other. Three parts matter:</p>

<p><strong>The interview format.</strong> Each piece follows a repeatable template: origin, product, customer acquisition, revenue, costs, and advice. Because the template is fixed, a reader can read twenty interviews and compare acquisition channels directly. That comparability is the archive's real value—not any single story.</p>

<p><strong>The numbers are self-reported.</strong> Founders state their own revenue and pricing. The archive's credibility rests on that being normal and expected, not on an editorial fact-check of every figure. Treat every dollar amount on the site as a founder's claim with a date attached, not as audited financials.</p>

<p><strong>The community layer.</strong> The interview archive feeds a forum where readers ask follow-up questions and post their own milestones. Readers arrive for a specific interview and stay for the discussion around it.</p>

<h2>How it was grown: channels and tactics</h2>

<p>Indie Hackers grew by making its subjects do the distribution. That is the single most transferable mechanic here.</p>

<p><strong>1. Interviewing people who already have an audience.</strong> Every interview hands a founder a reason to share a link with their own followers, newsletter and social accounts. The site's own following compounds on the back of its subjects' audiences. This is a well-known pattern in content businesses, and it is visible directly in the structure of the interview index: the archive is a list of other people's audiences.</p>

<p><strong>2. Publishing numbers nobody else published.</strong> At the time Indie Hackers was growing, candid revenue figures from small software businesses were scarce. The interviews made invisible businesses legible. Readers searching for "how much does a one-person SaaS make" found structured answers, which is the kind of long-tail query a small site can actually rank for.</p>

<p><strong>3. Making the format itself the brand.</strong> The repeated questions became recognisable. A reader can open any interview and know where the revenue discussion sits. Predictable structure is a retention mechanism: it rewards returning readers.</p>

<p><strong>4. Turning readers into contributors.</strong> The community lets people who were interviewed—and people who want to be—post their own progress. Growth comes from participation rather than from paid acquisition. Public discussions around Indie Hackers consistently emphasise the interviews as the entry point and the community as the reason people stay.</p>

<h2>Business model and pricing</h2>

<p>Indie Hackers' monetisation has changed over its life and is not fully disclosed in the interview archive. What can be stated without invention: the site has been operated as a commercial property, has carried sponsorship and advertising, and later offered paid membership tiers. Because current tiers, prices, and what each tier includes are not documented in the sources used here, this article does not quote prices. For anything a reader would pay for, check the official pricing on the site itself.</p>

<p>What <em>is</em> documentable is the revenue model inside the archive it publishes: the interviews describe businesses funded by subscriptions, one-time licence fees, and hosted service tiers. Those are the interviewees' models, not Indie Hackers'. Conflating the two is the most common error people make when citing this site.</p>

<h2>Startup cost and scale</h2>

<p>Indie Hackers began as a small, self-funded web project, and the interview archive is itself the record of what that kind of project costs: the founders interviewed describe building with contractors, off-the-shelf tools and modest hosting bills rather than venture funding. What the archive does not provide is a breakdown of Indie Hackers' own build cost, headcount history, or current revenue. Anyone quoting a specific figure for the site's revenue or acquisition price should be treated with suspicion unless they link to a primary source. The honest position is this: the archive documents hundreds of small businesses in detail, and the platform hosting it does not publish comparable disclosure about itself. Scale, for the reader's purposes, means the size of the archive—thousands of interviews and a long-running forum—not a dollar figure.</p>

<h2>What you can copy as one person</h2>

<p><strong>Pick a fixed question set and never change it.</strong> The interview template is the product. A consistent structure lets readers compare across entries, which is what no blog post does. Decide your six to eight questions before your first interview and hold the line.</p>

<p><strong>Interview people who already have an audience.</strong> Every subject is a distribution channel. Send the finished piece to the founder and make sharing easy. The compounding comes from their reach, not yours.</p>

<p><strong>Ask for numbers with a date attached.</strong> "Revenue as of this month" beats "revenue is growing." Self-reported and dated is honest; undated and vague is not.</p>

<p><strong>Say whose numbers they are.</strong> The biggest credibility risk in this niche is readers assuming you earn what your interviewees earn. Label every figure with its owner.</p>

<p><strong>Build the community after the archive, not before.</strong> The interviews give people a reason to arrive. An empty forum gives nobody a reason to stay. Announce the community once you have enough interviews that a reader has something to discuss.</p>

<p><strong>Don't publish prices you haven't verified.</strong> If you write up a founder's business and they say "our pricing changed," link to their pricing page and let it speak for itself.</p>

<h2>What doesn't work</h2>

<p><strong>Treating a directory of other people's success as your own.</strong> The most common failure in this niche is a site that aggregates income claims while publishing nothing about the operator. Readers notice, and the format's credibility—its only asset—evaporates. Transparency has to run both ways or it reads as marketing.</p>

<p><strong>Interviewing only the loudest founders.</strong> Founder-interview sites drift toward the same handful of well-known indie names, all with existing audiences, all telling the same story. The archive loses its comparative value. The long tail—the person with a modest, verified, boring business—is the scarce content.</p>

<p><strong>Building the forum first.</strong> Forums with no archive behind them start empty and stay empty. The interview archive is the traffic source; the community is the retention layer. Getting the order wrong is the standard failure mode of community-first one-person projects.</p>

<p><strong>Chasing breadth over depth.</strong> A site with 40 detailed, numbers-backed interviews outperforms one with 400 thin profiles. Readers can tell the difference immediately, and search engines increasingly can too.</p>

<h3>Can one person realistically start a founder-interview site?</h3>

The cost is time—interviewing, editing, and maintaining the consistency that makes the archive comparable. Revenue is not guaranteed and typically follows sponsorship or membership once readership exists.</p>
