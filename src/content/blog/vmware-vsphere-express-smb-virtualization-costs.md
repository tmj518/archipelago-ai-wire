## What happened

VMware announced vSphere Express on 4 November 2026. The company described it as a lower-end virtualization offering for organizations that require "cost-effective essential virtualization," according to Ars Technica. IT distributor TD Synnex lists the product at US$9,600 a year for running on up to 128 processor cores.

The product includes two basic capabilities. vMotion allows live migration of running virtual machines with improved migration speed and efficiency. High availability allows automated restart of failed virtual machines. Both features are part of the announcement.

The new product arrives after a gap. Some small and medium business users reported that VMware sales representatives previously told them that vSphere Standard, VMware's prior entry-level virtualization offering, was no longer available. That left SMBs without a clear entry-level option from VMware itself.

## Why now

The thread runs back two to three years, to the period after Broadcom acquired VMware and began restructuring the product line. SMB customers reported that entry-level options were being withdrawn, with sales representatives telling them vSphere Standard was no longer available. That left smaller organisations facing a choice between more expensive tiers or moving to rival platforms.

What surfaced this month is the response. vSphere Express is VMware's attempt to fill the entry-level gap it created. The pricing is a per-core subscription, which is a different model from the old perpetual licences that many Malaysian firms bought once and ran for years. The subscription model changes the cost structure: a firm pays every year instead of paying once.

The timing also matters because the migration wave is already running. Malaysian IT service providers have spent the past two years moving clients off VMware to alternatives like Proxmox and XCP-ng, which are open-source and free to licence. A priced VMware option gives those providers a reason to pause and compare before they finish a migration.

## So what

The gain lands on small Malaysian IT firms and their clients. A firm running a small server room on VMware now has a published price to compare against the cost of migrating. Migration is not free: it takes engineer time, testing, downtime and retraining. If the VMware subscription is cheaper than the migration project, staying becomes the rational choice.

The cost lands on the firms that already migrated. A business that moved off VMware in the past two years, because entry-level options were withdrawn, now faces a new price from the vendor it left. The migration cost is sunk. The new subscription does not undo that spending, and the firm must weigh whether the new price justifies switching back.

The third actor is the rival platform. Open-source hypervisors are free to licence, but they carry their own costs in support, expertise and compatibility. A priced VMware option changes the comparison: the question is no longer "free versus expensive" but "free plus migration risk versus a known subscription price."

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on Malaysian IT service providers and the small and medium businesses they serve. Malaysia has a large population of SMBs running modest server rooms, and a layer of IT firms that maintain those servers for them. Those firms are the ones who read a VMware price list and decide what to recommend.

The concrete carrier is the managed service provider. A Malaysian IT firm that maintains ten clients on VMware now has a number to put beside each client's renewal. For a client running under 128 cores, the US$9,600 annual subscription becomes a line item in a proposal, and the provider can compare it against the cost of a migration project. That comparison is the practical decision point.

The second carrier is the SMB owner who pays the bill. A business that was told its VMware option was gone now has a new choice. The subscription is priced in US dollars, which means the ringgit cost moves with the exchange rate, and that is a risk a Malaysian buyer carries that a US buyer does not.

The third carrier is the local IT job market. If more Malaysian firms stay on VMware, the skills that local engineers already hold remain valuable. If more firms migrate, the demand shifts toward open-source skills. The new product does not settle that question, but it changes the arithmetic behind it.
