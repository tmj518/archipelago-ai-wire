## What happened

The Federal Reserve Board, the central bank of the United States, finalised changes to its annual bank stress test on 30 September 2026. The press release announcing the decision says the changes "enhance the transparency and public accountability" of the stress test and "reduce volatility" in the capital requirements the test produces.

A stress test is a simulation. The Fed takes the largest US banks, imagines a severe economic crisis, and calculates how much money each bank would lose. If a bank's losses are too large relative to its capital, the Fed requires it to hold more capital. Capital is the money a bank keeps aside that is not lent out, invested or paid as dividends. It is the cushion that absorbs losses before depositors or the government do.

> **Capital requirement:** the minimum amount of money a bank must hold in reserve, set by a regulator, to absorb losses in a crisis. It is not a fine or a fee. It is a rule about how much of a bank's own money must sit behind its lending.

The final rule changes how that requirement is calculated. The press release does not give the new formula. It states the goal: less volatility, more transparency. The change is a rule about a rule, and the practical effect is that a bank's required capital will move less sharply from one year to the next.

## Why now

The thread is two to three years long. The Fed has been under pressure from banks and from Congress to explain how its stress test works. Banks argued that the test was a black box: the Fed told them how much capital to hold but not exactly why, and the number can jump sharply from one year to the next for reasons the banks can not see. That volatility made it hard for a bank to plan its lending, its hiring and its dividends.

The final rule is the end of that thread. The Fed has now committed, in a formal rule, to greater transparency and to smoothing the year-to-year swings. The timing this month is the publication of the final rule itself. The Fed had proposed the changes earlier, taken comments, and now issued the final version. A final rule is the point at which a regulatory change stops being a proposal and becomes a binding instruction.

The second reason it surfaced now is the broader regulatory calendar. The Fed runs its stress test every year, and the results feed into the capital requirements that take effect the following year. A final rule published in September sits in time for the next round of tests. The press release does not say which test cycle the rule applies to, and this piece does not guess.

## So what

The gain lands on the largest US banks and on the regulators who supervise them. A bank that can see how its capital requirement is calculated can plan. It knows roughly what its cushion will be next year, so it can decide how much to lend, how many people to hire and how much to return to shareholders. Less volatility in the requirement means less volatility in those decisions.

The cost lands on the banks' shareholders and on the public in a subtler way. A more predictable capital requirement is not a lower one. The Fed is not reducing the cushion; it is making the cushion steadier. A bank that holds a steadier cushion is a safer bank, but it is also a bank that holds more money out of circulation in some years than it would have under the old rule. The trade is safety for flexibility.

The third actor is the global banking system. The US stress test applies to US banks, but the largest US banks operate in dozens of countries, and the largest foreign banks with US operations are also tested. A change in how the Fed sets capital requirements for those banks changes how much capital they hold worldwide, because capital is fungible. A bank does not keep a separate cushion for each country; it manages one global pool.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on Malaysian banks that have US operations, and through them on their employees and borrowers at home. The largest Malaysian banking groups run international businesses, and some of them have offices, branches or subsidiaries in the United States. A US bank with Malaysian ownership, or a Malaysian bank with a US presence, sits inside the Fed's stress test if it is large enough.

The concrete carrier is the parent group's capital planning. When the Fed sets a steadier capital requirement for the US part of the group, the group's global capital pool moves less. That pool is the same money that supports lending in Malaysia. If the US requirement swings less, the group has less reason to shift capital across borders to meet it, and less reason to cut lending in one market to satisfy a regulator in another.

The second carrier is the employee. A bank that faces a sudden jump in its capital requirement has three ways to meet it: raise new capital, cut lending, or cut costs. Cutting costs includes jobs. A steadier requirement removes one reason for a sudden cost-cutting round. That is the job-stability link, and it is a direction, not a promise. The press release does not say that any Malaysian bank will hire or fire anyone.

The third carrier is the borrower. A bank that lends more steadily lends more predictably. A small business in Malaysia that borrows from a bank with US operations faces a lender whose capacity to lend is less likely to swing with a US regulatory number. The link is real but indirect: the Fed's rule changes the US requirement, the US requirement changes the group's global capital position, and that position feeds the Malaysian lending book.

## FAQ

**What is a stress test?**

A stress test is a simulation run by a regulator. The Federal Reserve imagines a severe economic crisis, such as a deep recession or a market crash, and calculates how much money each large bank would lose. It then compares those losses to the bank's capital, the money held aside to absorb losses. If the losses are too large, the Fed requires the bank to hold more capital. The test is run every year, and the results set the capital requirement for the following year. The final rule changes how that requirement is calculated, not whether the test is run.

**Which Malaysian banks are affected?**

The press release does not name any bank, Malaysian or otherwise. The rule applies to banks that sit inside the Fed's stress test, which are the largest US banks and the largest foreign banks with US operations. A Malaysian banking group with a US branch or subsidiary large enough to be tested would be affected. The effect on a Malaysian bank's Malaysian lending is indirect, running through the group's global capital pool. Bank Negara Malaysia, the central bank, has not issued a statement on the rule.

**When does the rule take effect?**

The press release does not state an effective date. A final rule from a US regulator typically takes effect after a set period, but the press release does not give one. The rule will apply to a future stress test cycle, and the Fed runs its test annually. The practical effect on capital requirements will show in the next round of results, which the press release does not date. A reader should watch the Fed's publication of the full rule text for the effective date.
