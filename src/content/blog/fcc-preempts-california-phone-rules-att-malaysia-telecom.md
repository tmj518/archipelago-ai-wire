## What happened

The US Federal Communications Commission, the federal regulator for telephone and broadband service, moved to preempt California's telecom rules. Preemption is the legal step by which a federal regulator sets aside a state's rules in a field it says it controls. The order clears the way for AT&T to stop offering basic phone service to new customers and to discontinue service for 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers on 1 June 2027.

The background is a state-by-state campaign. AT&T has received relief from carrier-of-last-resort obligations in 20 of the 21 states in its wireline service territory, according to the Ars Technica article on the FCC order, retrieved on 20 November 2026. California is the one state that has stood alone against the company.

The pressure point is money. California faces a choice between enforcing its telecom laws and receiving 1.42 billion US dollars in federal broadband deployment grant money, which is meant to reach 270,571 locations. Every figure in this article comes from that single Ars Technica report.

> **Carrier of last resort (COLR):** a legal duty that makes a phone company serve every household in a defined area, including the ones that are expensive to reach, and keep serving them at a regulated price. It is the obligation that stops a network operator from walking away from a rural line or an elderly customer because the line loses money. Relief from COLR means the company is no longer the provider of last resort in that area.

## Why now

The thread runs through the copper network. For a century, the telephone line into a house was a pair of copper wires carrying a voice call and, later, a slow internet connection. Fibre and mobile networks have replaced most of that traffic. What remains on copper is a shrinking set of customers, and the cost of maintaining a copper line does not fall as fast as the revenue on it. Every large telecom operator in a rich country has been retiring copper for the past decade.

The second thread is the legal one. COLR obligations were written for a world where the phone company was the only network. Once a cable or fibre operator serves the same street, the duty to serve everyone becomes a duty to serve the customers nobody else wants, at a price set years ago. AT&T has spent years asking state regulators to release it, and has won in 20 of 21 states. California is the holdout.

The third thread is the money. The federal broadband grant gives the federal regulator leverage: a state that insists on its own telecom rules puts 1.42 billion US dollars for 270,571 locations at risk. That is the mechanism that turned a state-by-state argument into a federal preemption this month.

## So what

The gain lands on AT&T. Relief from COLR removes a regulated obligation and lets the company move 199,000 customers off a network it no longer wants to run. The mechanism is straightforward: a line that must be served at a regulated price is a cost; a line that can be discontinued is a cost that ends.

The cost lands on the household still on the line. A customer in a rural area, an elderly customer with a landline and no mobile, a small business with an alarm system wired to copper: these are the people COLR was written for. When the obligation lifts, the service does not vanish on the same day, but the company's incentive to maintain it does. The customer is left to find a replacement, and the replacement is a different technology with a different price.

The third actor is the state regulator. California's choice is between two goods it cannot both have: its own consumer rules, and the federal money to build the network that replaces copper. That trade is the real story, and it is the one a Malaysian regulator will recognise.

## What it means for Malaysia

Malaysia runs its own copper retirement, and the same trade sits inside it. Telekom Malaysia has been migrating customers from copper to fibre for years, and the universal service obligation — the Malaysian version of COLR, funded through a levy on operators — is the mechanism that keeps a line available in areas a commercial operator would skip. The concrete carrier is the household in a rural district or a flats block where fibre has not reached, and the small business whose phone line, card terminal or alarm runs on copper.

The second carrier is the regulator, the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission. The US case shows the sequence a regulator faces: an operator asks for relief from the obligation, the regulator weighs the cost of the obligation against the benefit to the remaining customers, and the operator's alternative is to stop investing. The lesson is not that Malaysia should copy the FCC. It is that the obligation and the retirement have to be decided together, because a household that loses its line before fibre arrives has no fallback.

The third carrier is price. A customer moved from a regulated copper line to a fibre package pays the fibre price, and the difference lands on a monthly bill. ## For you

- **A household still on a copper landline:** the US case shows the sequence to watch. When an operator asks for relief from its obligation to serve you, the question to ask your provider is what replaces the line, at what price, and on what date. Ask before the copper is switched off, not after.
- **A small business with a card terminal, alarm or lift phone on copper:** these devices often run on the old line and are not always compatible with a fibre replacement. Check with the device supplier what the migration requires, because the cost of a failed card terminal at the counter lands on the shop, not the telecom operator.
- **A student or worker following telecom policy:** the useful comparison is not the headline but the trade. California is being asked to give up its own consumer rules for 1.42 billion US dollars and 270,571 connected locations. When a regulator here faces a similar trade, the question is who audits the coverage promise afterwards.
- **A rural or flats-block resident waiting for fibre:** the obligation that keeps your line alive is the same one an operator has an incentive to shed. The practical step is to register your address with your provider as a migration case, so the record shows a customer waiting rather than a line with no one on it.

## FAQ

**What did the FCC actually do, and why does it matter outside the United States?**
The FCC, the US federal regulator for telephone and broadband, moved to preempt California's telecom rules, clearing the way for AT&T to stop offering basic phone service to new customers and to disconnect 184,000 residential and 15,000 business customers on 1 June 2027. It matters outside the US because the underlying decision — an operator leaving a copper network and a regulator deciding who protects the customers still on it — is the same decision every country with a copper network faces, including Malaysia. The US case shows the sequence and the trade, not a template to copy.

**What is carrier of last resort, and does Malaysia have one?**
Carrier of last resort is a legal duty that makes a phone company serve every household in an area, including unprofitable ones, at a regulated price. AT&T has received relief from that duty in 20 of the 21 states in its wireline territory, with California the holdout. Malaysia's counterpart is the universal service obligation, funded through a levy on operators and administered by the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission. It performs the same function: it keeps a line available where a commercial operator would rather not serve. The two are not identical in law, and the Malaysian terms are set in Malaysian rules.

**What should a Malaysian household on a copper line do now?**
The practical step is to find out what replaces the line before it is switched off. Ask the provider what service will replace copper at your address, what it costs per month, and on what date the change happens. For a small business, check whether the card terminal, alarm or lift phone works on the replacement, because those devices are the ones that fail silently in a migration. Registering the address as a migration case puts a waiting customer on the record, which is the evidence a regulator uses when it weighs an operator's request for relief.
