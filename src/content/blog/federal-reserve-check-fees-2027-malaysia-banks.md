The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States. The Federal Reserve Banks are twelve regional institutions that act as the banking system's back office: they move money between banks, settle balances, and process checks drawn on US accounts. They charge the banks that use those services a published fee, and the price list is reset on a schedule.

> **Correspondent bank:** a bank in one country that holds an account with a bank in another country and clears payments through it on behalf of its own customers. A Malaysian bank that needs to clear a US dollar check does not deal with the Federal Reserve directly; it deals with a US correspondent bank, which in turn uses the Federal Reserve's services and pays the Federal Reserve's fees.

## Why now

The thread is a pricing cycle, not a single event. The Federal Reserve Banks publish a fee schedule for the services they sell, and they revise it on a forward-looking timetable so that the banks paying those fees can plan. The 12 November 2026 announcement sets the price that applies from 1 January 2027. That is roughly seven weeks of notice for the banks on the receiving end.

The second thread is the check service itself. Checks are a shrinking part of the American payments system, and the release pairs the new pricing with changes meant to streamline and adjust check services. Read together, the two halves of the announcement describe an operator resizing a service line while repricing it. The direction of the resizing is stated; the arithmetic behind it is not in the text retrieved.

The third thread is timing. A price list that takes effect on 1 January lands at the start of a calendar year, when banks on both sides of the Pacific set their own charges for the year ahead. A Malaysian bank that learns in November what its US correspondent will pay from January has a window to decide what it charges its own customers. That window is the reason this announcement is worth reading in Kuala Lumpur in November rather than in February.

## So what

The gain lands on the operator. The Federal Reserve Banks recover the cost of running a payment and check infrastructure that the banking system depends on. Repricing a service line is how an operator keeps that infrastructure funded when volumes and costs move.

The cost lands first on the banks that buy the services: US banks and credit unions, and the foreign banks that hold accounts with them. The mechanism is a pass-through. A correspondent bank pays the Federal Reserve's fee, adds its own margin, and charges the bank on the other side. That bank adds its margin and charges the customer. Each link in the chain is a business with its own cost base, and each decides how much of an upstream increase to absorb and how much to hand on.

What changes is not the existence of the fee but its level from a known date. What does not change is the structure: a Malaysian bank still needs a US correspondent to clear a dollar check, and the correspondent still needs the Federal Reserve. The announcement reprices a link in a chain that Malaysian payments already run through.

The honest limit is that the release, as retrieved, does not say how much the price moves. A large increase and a small one produce the same headline. The difference shows up in what a Malaysian bank charges for a dollar payment, and that number is set by the bank, not by the Federal Reserve.

## What it means for Malaysia

The carrier is the fee line on a cross-border payment. A Malaysian company that pays a US supplier by check, or a Malaysian family that receives a dollar remittance settled through a check, sits at the end of a chain that begins with the Federal Reserve's price list. The bank in the middle is a Malaysian bank with a US correspondent relationship, and its cost of clearing that item moves when the correspondent's cost moves.

Take a concrete case. A small exporter in Penang invoices a US customer and is paid by check drawn on a US bank. The check travels back through the exporter's Malaysian bank, which sends it for collection through its US correspondent. Every fee along that path is a deduction from what the exporter eventually receives. A repricing of the Federal Reserve's check services from 1 January 2027 changes one input into that deduction. The exporter does not see the Federal Reserve's name on any statement; the exporter sees a collection charge.

The same path carries household flows. A Malaysian working in the United States, or a family receiving support from a relative there, depends on dollar clearing that runs through the same correspondent relationships. The cost of that clearing is embedded in the exchange rate and the service fee the household is quoted.

The action for a Malaysian business is procedural and cheap. Ask the bank what it charges to collect a US dollar check, and ask whether that charge is changing for items cleared after 1 January 2027. A firm that moves several dollar payments a month has room to compare charges across banks, and a firm that can move a supplier onto a direct transfer rather than a check removes the check leg from the chain entirely.
