## What happened

Reuters reported on 26 November 2026, in an article carried by Free Malaysia Today, that rising public opposition in Japan has prompted a review of the country's data centre industry. About 60% of Japan's data centres are located in the Tokyo metropolitan area, and it is there that the objections have concentrated.

The article describes the objections in physical terms rather than in principle. In Kashiwa, two 31-metre-tall data centres owned by Hong Kong-based asset manager PAG stand in front of a resident's home. In Tokyo's Koto ward, Singapore-based Empyrion Digital is building a 51-metre data centre due for completion in 2029; the apartment building next to the site stands 3.7 metres away, and 1.2 million litres of heavy oil will be buried underground there. In Inzai, Britain's Colt Data Centre Services began construction this year on a 32-megawatt facility that will rise 11 metres in front of one resident's apartment.

Noise is the second complaint. Generators at an operating facility are tested whenever a new tenant moves in, and over the past two months they ran for some 110 hours combined.

The commercial backdrop is growth, not retreat. Consultancy JLL expects Japan's data centre market, measured by revenue, to grow to US$33.4 billion by 2030 from US$23.4 billion in 2024 — average annual growth of 6.7%, as reported by Reuters.

Every figure in this article comes from that single Reuters report, retrieved on 26 November 2026. The report does not name the review body, its terms of reference, its timetable, or any proposed rule. Those gaps are treated as gaps below.

> **Data centre:** a building full of servers that other companies rent space in. The servers run websites, payments, AI models and cloud software. The building needs three things at industrial scale: electricity to run the servers, cooling to stop them overheating, and diesel or heavy-oil generators to keep them alive when the grid fails. Those three needs are what neighbours end up living next to.

## Why now

The thread runs from a concentration decision made years ago. Japan put roughly six in ten of its data centres into one metropolitan area, close to its customers, its fibre routes and its engineers. That worked while the buildings were small and few. It stops working when the buildings become 31 metres, 32 megawatts and 51 metres tall, and when the land next to them is already full of apartments.

The second thread is the fuel. A data centre that must never go dark keeps a large store of liquid fuel on site for its generators. At the Koto site that store is 1.2 million litres, buried underground, next to a building 3.7 metres away. A buried tank of that size is a planning question, an insurance question and a fire-safety question at once, and it is the kind of question that turns a neighbour into an opponent.

The third thread is the testing regime. Generators are run whenever a new tenant moves in. In a building with many tenants, that is a recurring event, and 110 hours in two months is what it looks like from the other side of the fence. The complaint is not that the data centre exists. It is that the data centre is loud on a schedule the neighbour does not control.

What surfaced this month is the point at which those three threads met a review. The trigger is public opposition, as reported; the review is the response.

## So what

The gain lands on jurisdictions that can absorb the same building without the same fight. A data centre operator facing a planning review, a height restriction or a fuel-storage condition in Tokyo has a reason to look at sites where those conditions are lighter. That is the competitive signal, and it points at Johor.

The cost lands on the neighbour, and it is not a metaphor. A 51-metre building 3.7 metres from an apartment takes light, air and view. A generator test is audible hours. A buried fuel tank is a risk the neighbour carries without choosing it. None of that appears on the operator's invoice, which is exactly why planning rules exist to put a price on it.

The third actor is the Japanese review itself. A review that produces height limits, setback rules or fuel-storage conditions raises the cost of building in Tokyo and slows the pipeline. A review that produces nothing leaves the dispute where it is. The report does not say which way it is going, and that is the single most important unknown in this piece.

## What it means for Malaysia

The Malaysian carrier is the Johor data-centre corridor: the developers, contractors, equipment suppliers and land owners building and servicing the facilities that have taken a large share of Malaysia's approved investment. Malaysia approved RM350.4 billion in investments in nine months of 2026, with Johor taking a large slice of the data-centre portion — the numbers are set out in [our piece on the MIDA approvals](/blog/mida-approved-investments-9m-2026-johor-data-centres/).

The transmission path runs through siting, not through demand. Demand for data centres is not reduced by a Japanese review; if anything, a constrained Tokyo pushes projects toward cheaper, faster jurisdictions. What the Japanese case shows is the bill that arrives later. A facility built close to homes, with a large buried fuel store and a generator testing schedule, generates opposition on a delay. The delay is the danger: by the time the neighbours organise, the capital is spent and the building is standing.

For a Johor developer, the practical read is that the three Japanese flashpoints are all decided at the design stage, before the first pile is driven. Setback from the nearest home. Height relative to what is already there. Where the fuel sits and how it is contained. Generator testing frequency and the hours it is allowed to run. Each of those is cheaper to fix on a drawing than after a review.

The second Malaysian carrier is the electricity and water connection. A data centre is a very large electricity customer, and the local grid and water utility carry the obligation to serve it. That obligation is a cost borne by other customers on the same network. The Japanese review is a reminder that the neighbour's complaint is not only about noise; it is about who pays for the substation, the line and the water.

The third carrier is the investor's exit. A data centre is a long-lived asset valued on stable, predictable operation. A planning dispute in the operating years is a discount on that asset. A developer who can show a clean siting record, a contained fuel store and a published noise regime holds a better asset than one who cannot.
