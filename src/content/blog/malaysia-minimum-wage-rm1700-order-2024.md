## AI Summary

Malaysia's minimum wage is RM1,700 a month, up from RM1,500. It arrived in two steps: from 1 February 2025 for employers with five or more staff, and from 1 August 2025 for everyone else. The order also sets daily and hourly floors. It does not raise every low-paid worker automatically, and it does not control what prices do next.

## What happened

The wage floor for employees in Malaysia is **RM1,700 a month**, set by the Minimum Wages Order 2024, **P.U. (A) 376/2024**.

The order did not arrive everywhere on the same day. From **1 February 2025** it applied to employers with five or more employees, and to employers carrying out a professional activity classified under the Malaysian Standard Classification of Occupations. From **1 August 2025** it was aligned across the country, so the size of the employer stopped mattering.

That two-step design is the most important thing about this order, and it is the part usually left out of the announcement. It sits in [Work, Education and Family](/category/work-education-family/) because that is where its consequences land: a payslip, a roster, a household budget. A five-person shop and a five-hundred-person factory were covered six months apart.

## Why now

The order was gazetted in 2024 and its final step took effect on 1 August 2025, which means the policy is now fully in force rather than half in force. That date is the reason this piece exists: until it passed, a large share of small employers were still paying RM1,500 lawfully.

The Order also replaced a patchwork, and the desk's [sourcing rules](/about/) explain why a gazette number sits in the first paragraph rather than a ministry press release. Minimum wage in Malaysia had been set by earlier orders with their own thresholds and their own effective dates. Alignment means one number applies to an employer of any size, in any state, in the sectors the Order covers.

## So what

A wage floor is a price control on labour, and price controls work on the margin. Three effects follow, and only the first is arithmetic.

**First, the covered worker's basic pay rises.** Anyone who was lawfully paid RM1,500 and is now covered must be paid RM1,700 for the same hours.

**Second, the employer's cost per hour rises by more than the wage.** Malaysian employers pay statutory contributions on top of basic wages, so a higher basic wage carries a higher contribution bill. The order sets the wage; it does not set the total cost of employing someone.

**Third, employers adjust.** The known adjustments are fewer overtime hours, fewer staff doing more work, higher prices, delayed hiring, or machinery. Which one dominates depends on the sector and cannot be read off the order. A restaurant with thin margins and a software firm with a cleaner on contract respond differently to the same rule.

The daily and hourly rates are what make the order enforceable where nobody is paid monthly:

> For an employee who is not paid basic wages but is paid wages based only on piece rate, tonnage, task, trip or commission, the rate of monthly wages payable to that employee with effect from 1 August 2025 shall not be less than RM1,700.00.

That sentence is the Order's own wording, reproduced in the legal alert cited below, and it closes the obvious loophole: an employer who pays by the task rather than by the month still owes the monthly floor.

## What it means for Malaysia

For a household at the floor, the question is not whether the number went up but whether it went up faster than the things it buys. The order cannot answer that. What it does is remove one specific form of loss: an employer who wanted to pay less than RM1,700 for standard hours now cannot, and a worker who was paid RM1,500 has a document to point at.

For small employers, the six-month difference mattered more than the level. A business with four staff had until August 2025 to reprice, and the ones that did not are now exposed to arrears as well as to the wage difference, because underpayment is recoverable for the period it happened.

There is a boundary worth stating plainly. The Order does not apply to domestic servants, who are excluded by reference to section 2(1) of the Employment Act 1955 and the equivalent Sabah and Sarawak ordinances. A household employing a maid is outside this wage floor, and no reading of the Order changes that.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The monthly, hourly and daily rates, the two effective dates, the domestic-servant exclusion and the piece-rate sentence are all stated in the Order and reproduced in a published legal alert that cites it by number and links to the gazette.

**Inferred.** The adjustment story in this piece is reasoning from how wage floors work, not a measurement. No claim is made about how many workers received a raise, how many employers cut hours, or what happened to prices, because none of those numbers is cited here.

**What would change this.** A revision or replacement order would change every rate on this page, and the review date above exists for that. A measured study showing that covered employers absorbed the increase without any of the adjustments listed would weaken the mechanism section, not the rates.

## For you

- **If you are paid at or near the floor.** Check your basic wage, not your gross. Allowances, overtime and claims sit on top of the floor and do not substitute for it. If your basic wage for normal hours is below RM1,700, the shortfall is recoverable.
- **If you employ people.** The test is the basic wage for normal hours, converted correctly for daily and hourly staff. Keep the payroll record that shows the conversion, because that is the document a labour officer asks for.
- **If you are budgeting a household.** Treat the raise as nominal until you have compared it with your own price basket. The order fixes a wage; it does not fix rent, food or transport.

## The data point

RM1,700 a month is **RM65.38 a day** on a six-day week and **RM8.72 an hour**. Those three figures describe one obligation, and an employer who satisfies one while missing another is still underpaying.

## FAQ

### Does the RM1,700 minimum wage apply to every employer in Malaysia?

Yes, from 1 August 2025. The Minimum Wages Order 2024, P.U. (A) 376/2024, took effect on 1 February 2025 for employers with five or more employees and for employers carrying out a professional activity classified under the Malaysian Standard Classification of Occupations. From 1 August 2025 the order was aligned for all employers regardless of headcount, so the number of staff a business has no longer changes the floor it must pay. Domestic servants remain outside the order by reference to section 2(1) of the Employment Act 1955 and the equivalent Sabah and Sarawak ordinances.

### What was the minimum wage before, and how much did it rise?

The previous floor was RM1,500 a month, and the current floor is RM1,700, a rise of RM200. For an employee working a standard month that is the whole change in basic pay. The order also sets the daily and hourly equivalents so that staff paid by the day or the hour are not left behind: RM65.38 a day on a six-day week, RM78.46 on five days, RM98.08 on four days, and RM8.72 an hour. Those conversions matter because a monthly figure alone is easy to fudge in a workplace that pays by attendance.

### Does the minimum wage cover allowances, overtime and commission?

The floor applies to basic wages for normal hours. Allowances, overtime payments and expense claims sit on top and cannot be counted toward the RM1,700. For staff paid only on piece rate, tonnage, task, trip or commission, the order states that monthly wages must still not fall below RM1,700. That closes the route of paying a worker per task and letting a slow month produce a wage below the floor. If your contract converts the monthly figure into a daily or hourly rate, check the arithmetic against the table in this article.
