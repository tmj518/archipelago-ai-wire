## AI Summary

A Chinese steel shipment into the EU can carry two charges on one tonne: a CBAM carbon cost and, outside the quota, a safeguard duty of 50 per cent of CIF. They share no base. The carbon cost is knowable to the cent from a published formula; the duty tracks your invoice. Which is larger flips by product.

## What happened

Two EU instruments now apply to the same tonne of imported steel, and they were designed by different authorities for different purposes.

The **carbon border adjustment mechanism** charges importers for the embedded emissions in covered goods, minus a free-allocation adjustment. For Chinese hot-rolled coil under CN 7208, that comes to **EUR 163.53 a tonne** at the Q1 2026 certificate price of EUR 75.36 per tonne of CO2e, as set out in [the per-tonne CBAM breakdown](/blog/eu-cbam-cost-per-tonne-chinese-steel-aluminium-2026/).

The **steel safeguard**, replaced by Regulation (EU) 2026/1384 and applying from 1 July 2026, sets a tariff-rate quota of **18,345,922 tonnes** across the covered categories. Inside the quota a shipment enters duty-free. Outside it, the duty is **50 per cent of CIF**, doubled from 25 per cent.

Two charges, one tonne, and no rule that nets them against each other. Both are payable.

> **The two charges share nothing except the tonnage.** The carbon charge is a function of emissions per tonne of product, adjusted for free allocation and multiplied by a certificate price that the Commission publishes quarterly. The quota duty is a function of money per tonne of product — your invoice. One is an environmental instrument, the other a trade defence measure, and they meet only on the customs declaration.

## Why now

Because the quota side changed on 1 July 2026 and the carbon side changes every quarter, and the two moved in opposite directions in terms of predictability.

The safeguard duty went from 25 to 50 per cent. That is a doubling of a rate that applies to the full CIF value, which means its absolute size per tonne is a straight line through your invoice price.

The carbon cost did not move much at all between the first two quarters of 2026. At the Q1 certificate price of EUR 75.36 the cost for hot-rolled coil is EUR 163.53 a tonne; at the Q2 price of EUR 75.28 it is EUR 163.35. A certificate price movement of eight cents moved the per-tonne charge by eighteen cents.

So the volatility sits on the value side, not the emissions side, and the value side is the one the importer controls.

**The carbon cost is the more precisely computable of the two, and it is the one most summaries quote.** That is backwards from a budgeting standpoint. A figure that is knowable to the cent invites planning; a figure that is 50 per cent of a number you have not finalised resists it.

At a CIF value of EUR 500 a tonne the two charges cross inside this table. Flat steel and unwrought aluminium sit below the quota duty; every fabricated product sits above it. **The spread is not marginal** — bolts carry EUR 428.24 against the same EUR 250, and electrical steel carries EUR 539.91.

Three things follow.

**The ranking is a property of the product, not of the regime.** A reader who takes away "the quota duty is the bigger charge on Chinese steel" will be wrong on five of these ten codes. A reader who takes away "CBAM is the smaller charge" will be wrong on the same five.

**Where they cross is a single number, and it is your number.** Setting the two expressions equal gives CIF value per tonne equal to twice the CBAM cost per tonne. For hot-rolled coil that is EUR 327 a tonne. For bolts it is EUR 856. Below it the quota duty is larger; above it the carbon cost is.

| Product | CBAM EUR/t | Crossover CIF EUR/t | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hot-rolled coil, 7208 | 163.53 | 327 | Most HRC trades above this, so CBAM dominates |
| Coated sheet, 7210 | 156.13 | 312 | Same direction |
| Aluminium profiles, 7604 | 295.50 | 591 | Sits near the middle of the range |
| Screws and bolts, 7318 15 | 428.24 | 856 | Almost every fastener trade sits below it |

**The more fabricated the good, the larger the carbon charge relative to the duty, because the default values scale with processing while the duty rate does not.** The safeguard charges 50 per cent of whatever you paid. The carbon mechanism charges for emissions, and fabrication is emissions-intensive in a way that a percentage of invoice value cannot track.

**And the duty-free route is the only way to remove either charge, and it is mostly closed.** Both charges disappear inside the quota. [China holds named allocations](/blog/eu-steel-safeguard-quota-2026-chinese-hrc-cost/) in four of the twenty-six categories — 4.B, 3.B, 1.B and 3.A — and the largest of them is 182,996.85 tonnes. The remaining categories fall back on a residual pool of 22,256.02 tonnes in category 1.A shared by every supplier without a named allocation, against Turkey's named 642,294.94 tonnes in the same category. **An importer who finds an allocation is not exposed to either charge on that tonnage. One who does not is exposed to both.**

## For you

- **Importers of flat steel and unwrought aluminium:** at typical CIF values the safeguard duty is your larger charge, and it is a straight percentage of what you paid. Negotiating the price down reduces it proportionally. Check first whether the category you import has a Chinese allocation, because that determines whether the duty applies at all.
- **Importers of fabricated steel and aluminium — fasteners, structures, profiles, foil, electrical steel:** the carbon cost is your larger charge, and it does not respond to price negotiation. It responds to CN classification, because the default values differ by up to three and a half times across the codes in the table. A misclassified line is the most expensive mistake in this area and the cheapest to check.
- **Anyone near the 50-tonne threshold:** the CBAM exemption is 50 tonnes of net mass per importer per calendar year, aggregated across every covered CN code and across cement, iron and steel, fertiliser and aluminium together. It does not apply per consignment and it does not apply per code. There is no equivalent exemption from the safeguard duty.
- **Anyone budgeting for 2026:** the carbon side produces no cash outflow this year. Certificates sell from February 2027 and the first surrender is due 30 September 2027. The safeguard duty, by contrast, is payable at import. That mismatch means a 2026 budget that captures one and not the other is wrong in the direction that flatters it.

## How we calculated this

Every figure is published; the crossover needs one input from you, the CIF value. The carbon cost comes from the corrected CBAM default values and benchmarks, and the quota duty is the regulation's rate applied to your invoice.

```text
carbon cost per tonne = chargeable tCO2eq/t x certificate price for the quarter
EUR 163.53            = 2.16995 x 75.36                  (CN 7208, Q1 2026)

quota duty per tonne  = CIF value per tonne x 50%
EUR 250.00            = 500 x 0.50

crossover CIF         = 2 x carbon cost per tonne
EUR 327.08            = 2 x 163.53                       (CN 7208)
EUR 856.48            = 2 x 428.24                       (CN 7318 15)
```

Substitute your own CIF value and your own CN code. The crossover is where the two charges are equal, so it tells you which one to attack first without needing to know the exact figure for either.

## The point

Both charges are real, both are payable, and neither nets the other off — but they are not equally knowable, and that difference is the practical one.

The carbon cost is fixed by a published formula, a yearly default value and a quarterly price. It is the same for every importer of the same code and it can be computed before a contract is signed. The safeguard duty is 50 per cent of whatever you agreed to pay, which makes it the larger charge on flat and unwrought products and the smaller one on anything fabricated.

The mistake to avoid is treating the widely quoted per-tonne carbon figure as the headline cost of importing Chinese steel. On the codes where the safeguard duty applies and the quota is exhausted, it is not the headline. It is the second charge, and on the cheapest products it is the smaller of the two.

## FAQ

### Which charge is larger on Chinese steel entering the EU, CBAM or the safeguard duty?

It depends on the product and the CIF value, and the answer flips between them. At a CIF value of EUR 500 a tonne, the safeguard duty is a flat EUR 250. CBAM ranges from EUR 144.13 a tonne for unwrought aluminium to EUR 539.91 for grain-oriented electrical steel. Flat steel and unwrought aluminium sit below the duty; every fabricated product sits above it. There is no single answer, which is why the per-tonne carbon figure should not be quoted as the cost of importing.

### How do I work out where the two charges cross over for my product?

Double the CBAM carbon cost for your CN code. That gives the CIF value per tonne at which the two charges are equal. For hot-rolled coil at EUR 163.53 a tonne, the crossover is EUR 327.08 a tonne. For screws and bolts at EUR 428.24, it is EUR 856.48. Below the crossover the safeguard duty is larger; above it the carbon cost is. The carbon figure is the only input you need, and it comes from your classification rather than your invoice — so if the crossover lands uncomfortably close to your actual CIF, the thing to check is whether you are on the right code at all.

### Does the tariff-rate quota remove both charges or only one?

Both, on the tonnage that enters inside the quota. A shipment admitted within the duty-free quota pays neither the safeguard duty nor, where the CBAM exemption does not apply, does the quota affect the carbon charge — the two are separate instruments and the quota only removes the safeguard duty. The practical point is that China holds named allocations in four of the twenty-six categories, so for most Chinese steel there is no in-quota route to use, and the residual pool available instead is 22,256.02 tonnes shared across every supplier without a named allocation.

### Why is the carbon charge larger on fabricated steel than on flat steel?

Because the CBAM default value scales with processing and the safeguard rate does not. The safeguard charges 50 per cent of CIF whatever the good is. The carbon mechanism charges for embedded emissions, and the published default values reflect how much more emissions-intensive fabrication is. Hot-rolled coil carries a default value of 3.187 tCO2eq per tonne; screws and bolts carry 6.375, roughly double. Fabricating a good roughly doubles its carbon charge while leaving the duty rate untouched, so the crossover moves up with every processing step.
