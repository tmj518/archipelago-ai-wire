> **Interchange:** the fee a merchant's bank pays a cardholder's bank when a customer pays by card, and which the merchant's bank passes on to the merchant inside the fee it charges for accepting cards. The network — the company whose logo is on the card — sets the interchange rate. A merchant never sees the line item; it sees the total merchant discount rate, which bundles interchange with the network's own fee and the bank's margin.

The report's structure is the useful part for a Malaysian reader, because it separates the parties a merchant's fee actually pays.

## Why now

The thread is a two-year cycle, not a single event. A biennial report published on 19 December 2025 sits inside a sequence: an earlier edition, this one, and the next. Each edition puts fresh numbers on the same set of questions — who issues debit cards, which networks carry them, and what the transactions cost. The value of the document comes from the repetition. A merchant or a researcher can compare one edition against the last.

The second thread is the disclosure itself. The Federal Reserve Board collects this information from large issuers and networks and publishes a summary. That is a regulatory act: the data exists in public because a central bank required it and then wrote it up. The report is not a market survey commissioned by an industry body. It is a document a public institution produces on a fixed schedule.

The third thread is timing. The report surfaced in the retrieved record in November 2026, close to a year after publication. That gap is normal for a reference document: it is not news on the day it appears, it becomes useful when someone needs a benchmark. A Malaysian merchant reviewing card costs has had this US document available since December 2025 and almost certainly has not seen it, because nothing in the Malaysian card-acceptance process points to it.

## So what

The gain lands on the party that holds the information. In the United States, a merchant, a journalist or a regulator can open a public document and see the categories that make up card acceptance costs. That changes the negotiation: a merchant arguing about a fee has a reference point produced by a central bank rather than a number supplied by the party charging the fee.

The cost lands on the merchant who negotiates without that reference. A small shop accepting debit and credit cards pays a merchant discount rate set by its acquiring bank, which bundles interchange, the network fee and the bank's margin. The shop does not see the split. When the bank quotes a rate, the shop has no independent public document to compare it against. The asymmetry is structural: one side of the negotiation holds the cost breakdown, the other holds a total.

The third actor is the network. A network sets interchange and its own fee, and it competes for issuers by offering them a share of interchange. That competition runs between networks, and the merchant is not a party to it. The mechanism is a two-sided market: the network prices to attract cardholders and issuers on one side, and the merchant side pays. A public report on the transaction data is one of the few ways an outsider sees the shape of that market.

## What it means for Malaysia

The Malaysian carrier is the small merchant who accepts debit and credit cards, and the acquiring bank that prices the service to that merchant. A shop in a neighbourhood, a restaurant, an online seller — each pays a merchant discount rate, and each negotiates it with a bank that holds the cost breakdown. Malaysia has no public document equivalent to the Federal Reserve Board's biennial report in the material retrieved for this article. That absence is the Malaysian consequence: the benchmark exists in the United States and does not exist here.

The second carrier is the payment network itself. Malaysian debit transactions run across networks whose interchange rates are set commercially, not published by a central bank on a two-year cycle. A merchant comparing a domestic debit rate against an international scheme rate is comparing two commercial quotes, not two public benchmarks. The US report shows what a public benchmark looks like; it does not supply a Malaysian number, and this article does not invent one.

The third carrier is the cost that reaches a household. Card acceptance is a cost of doing business, and a cost of doing business sits inside the price on the shelf. A merchant who cannot see the components of the fee cannot argue about them, and a cost that cannot be argued about is a cost that passes through. The transmission is slow and small per transaction, which is exactly why it goes unexamined.
