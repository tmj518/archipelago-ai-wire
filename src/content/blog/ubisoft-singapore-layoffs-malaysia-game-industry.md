## Why now

The thread here is not a single month. Large publishers have spent the last two to three years tightening how many projects they fund and how many studios they keep on contract, and a studio that depends on one publisher's pipeline feels that tightening first. The CNA report places the Ubisoft cut inside that pattern without quantifying it.

What surfaced this month is the Singapore number. A regional studio hub cutting a fifth of its local workforce is a visible marker for anyone in Southeast Asia who takes contract work from publishers abroad. Malaysia sits close to that hub: Malaysian studios compete for the same outsourced art, animation, quality-assurance and co-development work, and Malaysian developers move between Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Johor and Singapore for jobs.

The second reason is timing within the publisher's own cycle. A publisher reviewing its slate decides which projects continue and which do not, and staffing follows that decision with a lag. The report does not describe Ubisoft's slate, so the specific trigger stays outside what the document supports. What the document does support is the size of the cut and the share of the local workforce it represents.

## So what

The gain from a cut like this lands on studios that are hiring. When a publisher releases experienced staff into the regional market, studios with open roles and funded projects can recruit people they would otherwise have waited years to find. The mechanism is straightforward: a pool of available, trained workers appears in one city, and employers nearby draw from it.

The cost lands on the workers themselves and on the studios that were counting on the same contracts. A developer whose job ends with a publisher's decision has no control over the slate review that produced it. A studio that built its capacity around one publisher's pipeline faces the same exposure one step removed: fewer contracts offered, longer gaps between them, and pressure on the rates it can quote.

The third effect is on the next cohort. Students choosing a course and juniors choosing a first employer read layoff numbers as a signal about stability. A visible cut at a well-known studio shapes those choices, and the effect arrives with a delay of a year or more, when the intake into the industry shrinks.

The framework that fits is concentration risk. A studio whose revenue comes mostly from one publisher is exposed to that publisher's decisions. A studio whose revenue is spread across several clients, or that also sells its own products directly to players, absorbs a single lost contract without cutting its team. That is a position to defend, not a guarantee: spreading revenue costs management time and does not remove the risk that all clients tighten at once.

## For you

- **A developer weighing a move to a regional hub:** ask which project and which publisher the role sits under, and how long the contract runs, before accepting. The 70-job cut at one studio is the reason to ask, not a reason to stay put.
- **A small studio owner with one large client:** treat that client as a concentration risk and price the next contract with a plan for the gap if it is not renewed. A second client or a product sold directly to players changes what a lost contract costs you.
- **A student choosing a course or a first employer:** read the layoff number alongside the skills it affects. Contract work for publishers abroad is one path into the industry; studios that own their products are another, and the two carry different exposure.
- **A freelancer taking outsourced art or animation work:** keep more than one client active and keep your own records of delivered work, so a publisher's slate review does not leave you with a single income stream and no paper trail.
