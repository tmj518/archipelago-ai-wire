## AI Summary

Budget 2027 proposes a 35 per cent Socso matching incentive for e-hailing and p-hailing workers, rising to 50 per cent if platform companies also contribute, and 70 per cent for 200,000 self-employed people in 17 sectors. The government and Grab Holdings will share an RM160 million package. Projected median net income rises by up to RM227 a month for e-hailing drivers and RM100 for p-hailing riders, with EPF matching of up to RM600 a year against a RM6,000 lifetime limit. The contribution base is not published, so a worker cannot yet compute the ringgit cost.

## What happened

Budget 2027 sets out a matching incentive for the social security contributions of gig workers. The government will pay 35 per cent of the Socso contribution made by an e-hailing or p-hailing worker. That share rises to 50 per cent if the platform company also contributes towards the worker's Socso contribution.

A separate tier covers other self-employed people. The government will extend a 70 per cent matching incentive to 200,000 self-employed individuals across 17 sectors where Socso participation is not mandatory.

The package has a corporate partner. The government and Grab Holdings Limited have agreed to share the cost of an RM160 million package for e-hailing and p-hailing workers.

The budget also carries income projections. Median monthly net income for e-hailing drivers is projected to increase by up to RM227, and p-hailing delivery workers by up to RM100 a month. For retirement savings, Budget 2027 provides a matching incentive of up to RM600 a year for EPF contributions by e-hailing drivers and p-hailing riders, subject to a lifetime limit of RM6,000.

Every figure in this article comes from the Malay Mail article published on 23 November 2026 and retrieved on the same day. The article does not publish the contribution base on which the percentages are applied, the date the framework takes effect, or the split of the RM160 million between the two parties.

> **Matching incentive:** a payment in which the government adds a share on top of money the worker puts in. If a worker contributes RM100 and the matching rate is 35 per cent, the government adds RM35, so RM135 reaches the fund while the worker has paid RM100. The rate is a share of the contribution, not a share of income.

## Why now

The thread runs through the size of the workforce. An estimated 600,000 Malaysians earn a living through e-hailing and p-hailing. That is a labour force larger than many Malaysian towns, and it sits outside the ordinary employer-employee arrangement in which a company registers a worker, deducts a contribution and remits it. A rider is not on a payroll. The contribution has to be made by the worker, and the incentive is the government's way of making that worthwhile.

The second thread is the platform. The 50 per cent tier exists only if the platform company contributes. That turns a government subsidy into a negotiation with a private firm, and the RM160 million shared package with Grab Holdings is the first visible result of that negotiation. The design rewards a platform that pays in and leaves a platform that does not at the 35 per cent tier.

The third thread is the calendar. The framework is to be finalised in early 2027, which means the rates are proposals now. A worker reading this in November 2026 is looking at a design, not an enrolment form.

## So what

The gain lands on the worker who was previously uncovered. Socso is Malaysia's social security organisation, which pays out for employment injury and invalidity. A rider who is injured on a delivery and has no contribution has no claim. The matching incentive lowers the price of joining, and the 50 per cent tier lowers it further where the platform cooperates.

The cost lands on the same worker, because a matching incentive is not a waiver. The worker still pays a share of the contribution. The government pays the rest. The ringgit amount the worker pays depends on the contribution base, and the base is not in the published material. A worker cannot yet compute the monthly deduction.

The third actor is the platform. A platform that contributes moves its workers to the 50 per cent tier and shares the cost of the RM160 million package. A platform that does not contribute leaves its workers at 35 per cent. The incentive is structured so that the platform's own decision changes the worker's cost, and that is a design choice worth watching when the framework is finalised.

## What it means for Malaysia

The Malaysian carrier is the rider and the driver themselves: the person on a motorcycle in the rain between 11am and 2pm, and the person behind the wheel on a Friday night. The concrete change is a deduction from their earnings and a claim they did not have before.

The second carrier is the platform operator's cost line. A contribution towards worker Socso is a new expense per active worker, and the RM160 million shared package is the first published number attached to it. How a platform absorbs that expense, whether through incentives, fares or the number of active accounts, is not stated in the document.

The third carrier is the household budget of a gig worker's family. The EPF matching of up to RM600 a year against a RM6,000 lifetime limit is small against a working lifetime, but it is the first retirement channel aimed at this group. A rider who contributes RM600 a year receives up to RM600 more, and the lifetime cap means the benefit ends after the cap is reached.

## How we calculated this

The directive supplies the matching rates and the projected income gains, but not the contribution base. The arithmetic below therefore shows the shape of the calculation and the one figure that can be derived from the published numbers: the implied contribution base behind the RM227 projection, if the projection is read as the government's matching share alone. That reading is an assumption, labelled as one.

```text
Given:
  matching rate, base tier            = 35 per cent
  matching rate, with platform        = 50 per cent
  matching rate, other self-employed  = 70 per cent
  projected median net income gain, e-hailing = up to RM227 per month
  projected median net income gain, p-hailing = up to RM100 per month
  EPF matching                        = up to RM600 per year
  EPF lifetime limit                  = RM6,000
  workers in e-hailing and p-hailing  = 600,000

Step 1 - implied contribution base, if RM227 is the 35 per cent share:
  base = 227 / 0.35 = 648.57
  So a monthly contribution of about RM649 would produce a RM227 government share.

Step 2 - same base at the 50 per cent tier:
  government share = 648.57 x 0.50 = 324.29
  worker share     = 648.57 x 0.50 = 324.29

Step 3 - same base at the 35 per cent tier:
  government share = 648.57 x 0.35 = 227.00
  worker share     = 648.57 x 0.65 = 421.57

Step 4 - EPF lifetime cap in years at the maximum annual match:
  years = 6,000 / 600 = 10

Step 5 - shared package per worker, if spread evenly across the sector:
  per worker = 160,000,000 / 600,000 = 266.67
```

The calculation shows why the missing base matters. At a base of about RM649 a month, the worker's own share is RM422 at the 35 per cent tier and RM324 at the 50 per cent tier. The difference between the two tiers is about RM97 a month to the worker. If the actual contribution base is lower or higher, every one of those figures moves with it. The RM227 projection is described in the source as an increase in median net income, which is a different quantity from a government matching share, so Step 1 is an illustration of scale and not a statement of what the government will pay.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The Malay Mail article of 23 November 2026 states the 35 per cent, 50 per cent and 70 per cent matching rates, the 200,000 self-employed people across 17 sectors, the RM160 million package shared by the government and Grab Holdings, the projected income gains of up to RM227 and up to RM100 a month, the EPF matching of up to RM600 a year against a RM6,000 lifetime limit, and the estimate of 600,000 workers. Those are the only figures used here, and each traces to that single article.

**Inferred.** The reading that the 50 per cent tier is a bargaining lever aimed at platform companies is an inference from the structure of the proposal, not a statement in the source. The reading that the RM160 million package is connected to the platform contribution tier is also an inference; the article reports both facts but does not state the link. The implied contribution base in the calculation above is an assumption built to show scale, not a published rate.

**What would change my mind.** A published contribution schedule from Socso, or a Budget 2027 appendix setting the contribution base and the effective date, would replace the assumption in the calculation with a real figure. A statement from Grab Holdings or another platform on whether it will contribute, and at what rate, would settle the tier question. A breakdown of the RM160 million between government and company would show who is paying for what.

## For you

- **An e-hailing driver doing this full time:** the 35 per cent tier applies to your contribution unless your platform also pays in, in which case the government share rises to 50 per cent. The projected median net income gain is up to RM227 a month, and the EPF match is up to RM600 a year against a RM6,000 lifetime cap. Ask your platform whether it will contribute before you budget the gain.
- **A p-hailing rider working evenings and weekends:** the projected median net income gain is up to RM100 a month, and the same EPF match applies. The contribution base is not published, so the monthly deduction is not yet known. Treat the RM100 as a projection, not a payslip line.
- **A self-employed person outside the gig platforms:** if you work in one of the 17 sectors where Socso participation is not mandatory, the 70 per cent matching rate is the tier that applies to you, and the scheme is sized for 200,000 people. Check with Socso whether your sector is on the list before assuming you qualify.
- **A household budgeting around a gig earner's income:** the incentive changes the cost of cover, not the size of the fare. The gain arrives only if the worker enrols and contributes. Until the framework is finalised in early 2027, the safest planning assumption is the current income.

## FAQ

**What is the Socso matching incentive for gig workers in Budget 2027?**
The government will pay 35 per cent of the Socso contribution made by an e-hailing or p-hailing worker. That share rises to 50 per cent if the platform company also contributes towards the worker's Socso contribution. A separate tier gives a 70 per cent matching incentive to 200,000 self-employed individuals across 17 sectors where Socso participation is not mandatory. The matching rate is a share of the contribution, not a share of income, so the ringgit amount depends on the contribution base, which the published material does not state. The framework is to be finalised in early 2027.

**How much more will an e-hailing driver or p-hailing rider earn?**
Budget 2027 projects that median monthly net income for e-hailing drivers will increase by up to RM227, and for p-hailing delivery workers by up to RM100 a month. Those are projections published with the budget, not guaranteed amounts, and they are described as median figures, which means half of workers would gain less than the stated amount if the projection holds. Separately, the EPF matching incentive is up to RM600 a year for e-hailing drivers and p-hailing riders, subject to a lifetime limit of RM6,000.

**What is the RM160 million package shared with Grab Holdings?**
The government and Grab Holdings Limited have agreed to share the cost of an RM160 million package for e-hailing and p-hailing workers. The published article does not state how the cost is split between the two parties, what the package contains, or how it is distributed to workers. Spread evenly across the estimated 600,000 workers in the sector, RM160 million is about RM267 per worker, but the article gives no basis for assuming an even spread. The package sits alongside the Socso matching tiers rather than replacing them.

## The point

The design is clear and the arithmetic is not. A rider can see three matching rates and two income projections, and cannot yet see the contribution base that turns a percentage into a monthly deduction. The useful move before early 2027 is to ask the platform one question: will it contribute, and so move its workers from the 35 per cent tier to the 50 per cent tier. That single answer changes the worker's share more than any other published detail.
