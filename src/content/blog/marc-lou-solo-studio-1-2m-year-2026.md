## So what

The gain lands on the builder who treats products as a portfolio. If one product in ten works, and the working one earns enough to cover the nine that do not, the portfolio survives. That is the mechanism: many small bets, each cheap to start, with the winners paying for the losers. The 43 products are not 43 successes. They are 43 attempts, and the revenue panel shows which ones cleared the bar.

The cost lands on focus. Running several products at once means splitting attention across support inboxes, billing systems, bug reports and marketing. A solo builder with zero employees absorbs all of that personally. The stated 90 per cent gross margin describes the money left after the direct cost of delivering the software, which for hosted software is mostly servers and payment fees. It does not describe the hours.

The third actor is the reader who sees $1,234,900 and concludes that shipping more products is the path to it. The panel does not show what the 40 products outside the top few earn, and it does not show what was spent building them. The visible structure is a portfolio. The invisible part is the five years and the failures inside the 43.

## FAQ

**Is the $1,234,900 figure verified?**
No. It is self-reported on Marc Lou's personal site, read on 7 November 2026, and there is no audited statement or payment-processor export in the material available. The product-level monthly figures on the same page are internally consistent with the annual total, which is a reason to take them seriously as a record of what the site claims, not as proof of what was banked. **Why does a portfolio of small products beat one big bet?**
Because the cost of a failed attempt is small and the cost of a single failed bet is total. If each product is cheap to build and cheap to host, a builder can afford several attempts, and one that works pays for the rest. The risk is spread across independent customer bases, so a decline in one product does not end the business. The trade-off is attention: a solo builder with no employees carries every product's support and maintenance personally.

**What can a Malaysian reader actually copy?**
The structure: small products, dollar pricing where the customer is overseas, low hosting costs, and a shipping cadence that produces several attempts rather than one. What does not transfer is the income. The $1,234,900 is the sum of a five-year run with 43 attempts behind it, and the panel does not show what the smaller products earn or what they cost. A Malaysian builder should copy the cadence and the pricing discipline, and expect the totals to look different.
