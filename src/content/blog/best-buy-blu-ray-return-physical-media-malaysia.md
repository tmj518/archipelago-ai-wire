## Why now

The thread runs in three steps over three years. In October 2023, Best Buy announced it would leave disc sales, and the exit took effect in 2024. That decision was a bet that streaming had won and that shelf space in a consumer electronics store was worth more given to chargers, speakers and smart home devices. Then, in 2026, the same retailer began selling Blu-rays directly again.

The reason the reversal surfaced this month is the size of the returning range. Fifteen titles is not a category reset. It is a test: enough stock to see whether customers buy, small enough that unsold copies are a rounding error. A retailer re-entering a category it abandoned does so in stages, and the first stage is measured in shelf-feet, not aisles.

The third thread is the format split inside the decline. DEG data shows total US disc spending down 9.3 percent from 2024 to 2025 while 4K Blu-ray spending rose 12 percent over the same period. The mass-market DVD buyer is leaving. The buyer paying a premium for the best available picture and sound is staying, and spending more. A retailer watching that split sees a smaller market with a more valuable customer inside it.

## So what

The gain lands on the manufacturer that presses and packages premium discs, and on the retailer that holds the shelf. A shrinking category with a rising premium segment is a profitable place to stand if the fixed costs are already sunk. Best Buy already has stores, a website and a delivery network. Adding 15 titles costs it very little; the question it is testing is whether the premium buyer will come to Best Buy for them or buy elsewhere.

The cost lands on the generalist retailer that stayed in the category through the decline. A shop that carried a wide DVD range through years of falling spending now faces a competitor re-entering with a narrow, curated, premium range and the marketing weight of a national chain. The wide range becomes a liability: more stock, slower turns, thinner margin.

The third actor is the streaming service. Every disc sold is a title a household owns outright, and a household that owns a film has less reason to keep paying a monthly subscription to watch it. The mechanism is small at 15 titles. It becomes real if other retailers read the same DEG split and follow.

## What it means for Malaysia

Malaysia does not press Blu-ray discs at commercial scale, so the local supply is imported. That makes the Malaysian channel a distribution one: a title reaches a Malaysian collector because a distributor here ordered it, or because the collector ordered it from abroad. The US is the largest market for English-language discs, and a US retailer expanding its direct range is a signal to the distributors and importers who decide what appears on a Malaysian shelf.

The concrete carrier is the specialist shop and the online importer. A shop in a Malaysian city that stocks 4K Blu-ray titles buys in small quantities and holds inventory risk on every copy. When a major US retailer re-enters the category, two things change for that shop. Availability of new releases improves, because a larger US order book supports bigger pressing runs. And price pressure arrives, because a Malaysian buyer comparing a local shelf price against a US retail price plus shipping has a new reference point.

The second carrier is the household that already owns a 4K television. A 4K set bought in Malaysia during the past few years delivers its best picture from a 4K disc or a premium streaming tier. A household weighing a monthly subscription against buying the films it rewatches now has a slightly better-stocked import market to buy from. That is a choice about a household bill, not a hobby.

The third carrier is the secondhand market. Discs hold resale value in a way that a streaming library does not, and a Malaysian collector who buys, trades and resells discs is trading in a market whose new-copy supply is set abroad.
