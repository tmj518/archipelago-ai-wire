## What happened

The European Commission published its sixth annual report on foreign direct investment screening on 14 October 2026. The Directorate-General for Trade and Economic Security reports that EU Member States notified 667 investments to the Commission in 2025, compared with 477 in 2024. The 2025 total was 40% higher, the highest annual rise since the cooperation mechanism was introduced.

Within that larger caseload, 88% of notified cases closed within 15 days. Another 12% required an in-depth security risk assessment. Commission opinions remained rare: the Commission issued opinions in less than 2% of cases in 2025, consistent with 2024 and 2023. The annual report records performance under the existing cooperation mechanism. The incoming regime is FISR, which applies from 17 January 2028.

> **Foreign direct investment screening** — the process through which EU Member States and the European Commission review investments from outside the EU for security and public-order risks before or around a transaction.

## Why now

The report gives importers a measured baseline while there is still planning time before FISR applies from 17 January 2028. What moved in 2025 was workload, not a published duty or fee. Member State notifications increased from 477 to 667. The arithmetic difference is 190 additional notifications, and 190 divided by 477 produces 0.3983, matching the reported 40% increase.

The important operational point is that speed held for most cases despite the larger workload: 88% closed within 15 days. At the same time, a 12% in-depth assessment share is large enough to require a contingency path in any acquisition timetable. The data support a two-track planning model: a fast historical track for most notified cases and an open-ended review track for escalated cases.

## So what

This article contains no per-unit duty rate because the source supplies no duty, fee, or monetary charge. The cost consequence is timing risk. An importer financing an EU acquisition, ownership restructuring, or post-closing integration has to carry capital, advisory, storage, and commercial-delay costs while screening remains open. The 2025 data show where that burden concentrated.

The fast-closure group is the dominant group. At 88%, the reported benchmark is 15 days. Applying that share mechanically to 667 notifications gives 586.96 cases. Applying the 12% in-depth share gives 80.04 cases. These are arithmetic products, not published whole-case headcounts, because the reported percentages are rounded. They still show the shape of the system: most files ended quickly, while a substantial minority entered a deeper process.

The opinion group is smaller. Less than 2% of 667 gives an arithmetic boundary of 13.34 cases. With whole cases, 13 is the largest integer below that boundary. The source does not publish the exact count, so 13 is only a boundary calculation. It confirms that formal Commission opinions were rare in aggregate, not that an individual transaction can ignore that outcome.

Use the following cost structure. Let **C** equal the importer's daily carrying cost and **D** equal the actual number of days in review. For the reported fast-closure pattern, the planning exposure is **15 × C**. For an in-depth assessment, the exposure is **D × C**. The incremental exposure beyond the 15-day benchmark is **(D − 15) × C**, and the per-unit allocation is that amount divided by affected units. The source supplies no value for D, so assigning a fixed monetary amount to the in-depth group would invent a deadline and a cost.

Importers should therefore put the 15-day figure in the base closing timetable only for files expected to track the 88% pattern. Files in the 12% group need long-stop financing, flexible completion conditions, and no promised release date. “Closed” is also the report's term; the report does not convert every closure into an approval label.

## FAQ

**Does the 88% share mean every EU FDI filing closes by day 15?**

No. The report says 88% of the 667 notified cases in 2025 closed within 15 days. The escalation group is material: 12% of notified investments required an in-depth security risk assessment. Those figures describe aggregate outcomes for one year, not a universal deadline for an individual transaction. The source also reports Commission opinions in less than 2% of cases, consistent with 2024 and 2023. Treat 15 days as the historical fast-closure benchmark, and keep a separate timetable path for escalation before FISR applies from 17 January 2028.

**What does the 12% in-depth assessment share mean for a closing schedule?**

It means a reported 12% of 2025 notifications entered a deeper security risk process. The annual report supplies no duration, fee, duty rate, or guaranteed end date for that process. An importer in this group cannot calculate a fixed screening cost from the supplied figures, and a 15-day closing assumption is unsupported. The practical arithmetic uses the importer's own daily carrying cost: financing, storage, advisory fees, and delayed commercial returns multiplied by the unknown number of review days. Transaction documents should keep financing and completion obligations open until the formal screening process ends.

**How should importers use the rise from 477 to 667 notifications before 2028?**

Use the rise as a workload signal, not as a duty rate. The Commission reports a 40% increase between 2024 and 2025, the highest annual rise since the cooperation mechanism began. A larger caseload raises the value of early filing and complete ownership information, but the report does not link volume to a specific future processing time. The 88% share shows most 2025 cases still closed within 15 days. At the same time, the 12% escalation share requires a contingency plan. Importers with EU acquisitions or ownership restructuring should align the filing plan with the FISR application date of 17 January 2028.
