## AI Summary

Malaysia's population is ageing. DOSM's Vital Statistics Time Series 2010-2019 shows live births fell 1.5% while deaths rose 29.1% over the decade. The total fertility rate dropped below replacement level from 2015, meaning each generation is not replacing itself. The crude death rate rose from 4.8 to 5.4 per 1,000 population, with the 70-74 age group recording the most deaths. This shift points to a smaller workforce supporting more retirees, affecting family planning decisions, healthcare demand, and government policy on pensions and services.

## What happened

The Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) published its Vital Statistics Time Series, Malaysia, 2010-2019, a ten-year record of births and deaths. The document shows two movements running in opposite directions.

Live births decreased 1.5 per cent, a drop of 7,554 births, from 501,303 births in 2010 to 493,749 births in 2019. The crude birth rate (CBR) — the number of live births per 1,000 people in the population — fell from 17.5 births in 2010 to 15.2 births per 1,000 population in 2019.

Deaths increased by 29.1 per cent, a rise of 39,616 deaths, from 136,109 deaths in 2010 to 175,725 deaths in 2019. The crude death rate (CDR) — the number of deaths per 1,000 people — rose from 4.8 deaths in 2010 to 5.4 deaths per 1,000 population in 2019.

The total fertility rate (TFR), which measures the average number of children a woman aged 15-49 would have in her lifetime, began recording below replacement level with 2.0 children starting from 2015. Replacement level is the number of children each woman needs to have for the population to hold steady without migration — commonly around 2.1 children per woman.

The age group 70-74 years recorded the highest number of deaths in 2019 with 20,504 deaths, or 11.7 per cent of all deaths that year.

The state breakdown shows wide variation. Terengganu recorded the highest crude birth rate with 23.7 births per 1,000 population, while Pulau Pinang recorded the lowest with 11.8 births per 1,000 population in 2019. Perak recorded the highest crude death rate with 7.6 deaths per 1,000 population, while W.P. Putrajaya recorded the lowest with 2.0 deaths per 1,000 population in 2019.

## Why now

This time series surfaced now because DOSM released it on 28 October 2026, giving the public a consolidated ten-year view. The data covers 2010 to 2019, the years before the pandemic disrupted health statistics. The release matters because it establishes the baseline trend that later years will be measured against.

The two-to-three-year thread behind this release is the steady decline in fertility. The TFR crossed below replacement level in 2015 and stayed there. That is not a single-year blip; it is a structural change in how many children Malaysian families are having. The birth rate decline is gradual — 1.5 per cent over ten years — but the fertility rate crossing below replacement is the more significant marker because it signals the population will eventually shrink without immigration.

The death rate rise is the other half of the story. A 29.1 per cent increase in deaths over ten years is faster than population growth, which means the population is ageing. More people are reaching older ages where death is more common. The 70-74 age group recording the highest number of deaths in 2019 confirms this: the large cohorts born in the 1940s and 1950s are now moving into their later years.

This release surfaces now because it gives policymakers and families a complete decade of evidence. The trend is not new, but the consolidated time series makes the direction unmistakable.

## So what

The judgement here is directional: Malaysia is moving from a young population to an older one, and the pace of that shift is now measurable.

Who gains? Younger workers entering the labour force in the next decade will face less competition for entry-level positions as the pool of new workers shrinks. Businesses serving older consumers — healthcare, retirement housing, financial planning — gain a growing market.

Who pays? The working-age population pays. A smaller share of workers will support a larger share of retirees through taxes that fund pensions, healthcare and public services. The dependency ratio — the number of dependents per working-age person — rises. That lands on the government budget and, eventually, on the taxpayer.

What changes? Family planning expectations change. A TFR of 2.0 children per woman means the average Malaysian family is not replacing itself. For a household, this is a private decision. For the country, it changes the calculus on everything from school construction to pension age.

The state variation matters for policy. Terengganu's crude birth rate of 23.7 births per 1,000 population is nearly double Pulau Pinang's 11.8. Perak's crude death rate of 7.6 deaths per 1,000 population is nearly four times Putrajaya's 2.0. A national policy that treats all states the same will miss these differences. States with older populations need more healthcare infrastructure; states with younger populations need more schools and jobs.

## What it means for Malaysia

The concrete carrier for this demographic shift is the healthcare system and the pension system.

On healthcare, the age group 70-74 years recorded the highest number of deaths in 2019 with 20,504 deaths. That age group will grow as the population ages. Hospitals, clinics and aged-care facilities will see rising demand for treatment of chronic conditions common in older patients — heart disease, diabetes, respiratory illness. The government's health budget will need to shift from maternal and child health toward geriatric care.

On pensions, the Employees Provident Fund (EPF) system depends on a flow of contributions from working members to fund withdrawals by retirees. A shrinking birth rate means fewer future contributors. The TFR falling below replacement level from 2015 means the cohorts entering the workforce in the 2030s will be smaller than the cohorts retiring. That gap puts pressure on the EPF's sustainability and on any government pension scheme.

For family planning, the data gives Malaysian couples a factual basis for their decisions. The TFR of 2.0 children per woman is below replacement. A couple choosing to have two children is making a choice that, repeated across the population, leads to a shrinking population. This is not a judgement on any family's decision; it is the arithmetic of population dynamics.

For businesses, the state-level data points to where demand is growing. Terengganu's high birth rate of 23.7 births per 1,000 population means demand for baby products, childcare and schools will hold up there. Pulau Pinang's low birth rate of 11.8 births per 1,000 population means that market is shrinking. Perak's high death rate of 7.6 deaths per 1,000 population signals a market for funeral services, estate planning and elder care.

The interest rate environment matters here too. With the central bank holding the OPR at 2.75 per cent as inflation averages 1.8 per cent, the cost of borrowing for long-term investments — hospitals, retirement homes, infrastructure — remains relatively low. ## FAQ

**What is the replacement level fertility rate and why does it matter?**
Replacement level is the average number of children each woman needs to have for a population to replace itself without migration. It is commonly around 2.1 children per woman. Malaysia's total fertility rate began recording below replacement level with 2.0 children starting from 2015. When fertility stays below replacement, each generation is smaller than the one before it. Over time, the population shrinks unless immigration makes up the difference. A shrinking population means fewer workers, fewer taxpayers and a larger share of older dependents. This is not an immediate crisis — the effects take decades to appear — but the direction is set by the fertility rate.

**Why did deaths rise 29.1 per cent while the population did not grow as fast?**
Deaths increased from 136,109 in 2010 to 175,725 in 2019, a rise of 39,616 deaths. The crude death rate rose from 4.8 to 5.4 deaths per 1,000 population. The rise is faster than population growth because the population is ageing. The large cohorts born in the 1940s and 1950s are moving into older age groups where death rates are higher. The age group 70-74 years recorded the highest number of deaths in 2019 with 20,504 deaths. As more people reach older ages, the number of deaths rises even if the death rate at each age stays the same.

**What does the difference between states mean for me?**
The state-level data shows Malaysia is not demographically uniform. Terengganu recorded the highest crude birth rate with 23.7 births per 1,000 population, while Pulau Pinang recorded the lowest with 11.8. Perak recorded the highest crude death rate with 7.6 deaths per 1,000 population, while W.P. Putrajaya recorded the lowest with 2.0. If you live in a state with a young population, demand for schools and entry-level jobs will hold up. If you live in a state with an older population, healthcare and elder services will grow. Businesses and policymakers should read the data for their own state, not the national average.
