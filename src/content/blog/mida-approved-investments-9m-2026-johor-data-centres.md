## What happened

The Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA) — the federal agency that approves and coordinates investment projects — reported on 17 November 2026 that Malaysia recorded RM350.4 billion in approved investments across the services, manufacturing and primary sectors in the first nine months of 2026. That is 20.7% higher than the RM290.3 billion recorded in the same period of 2025.

The approvals cover 4,387 projects. MIDA states that these projects are expected to create 152,090 jobs when fully implemented.

Johor was the top destination state at RM99.7 billion. Selangor followed at RM87.9 billion, Pulau Pinang at RM41.1 billion, W.P. Kuala Lumpur at RM37.3 billion and Sabah at RM19.5 billion. Within Johor, RM68.3 billion sat in services, mainly information and communications, and RM31.2 billion in manufacturing, led by electrical and electronics (E&E). Foreign investors put in RM81.4 billion of Johor's total, or 81.7% — the highest foreign share among the five leading states.

The United States was the largest foreign source at RM60.0 billion, up more than fivefold from RM11.3 billion in the same nine months of 2025. Exports rose 34.0% to RM1.571 trillion over the period, led by E&E products.

> **Approved investment:** a project that a federal or state investment authority has cleared to proceed, with a stated capital value. It is a commitment recorded on paper, not money already spent. A project can be approved and then delayed, scaled down or built over several years, so approvals measure the pipeline rather than the factory floor.

## Why now

The thread runs through the last two to three years of Malaysian investment policy, and it has two strands.

The first is the data-centre build-out. Johor's RM68.3 billion services figure is dominated by information and communications, the category that houses data centres — the warehouse-scale buildings that hold servers for cloud computing and artificial intelligence workloads. That concentration is why Johor, a state that was not a services hub a decade ago, now tops the state table.

The second is the screening layer that grew up around it. Johor has set up a data-centre screening council, a state-level body that reviews proposed data-centre projects before they proceed. The council exists because data centres consume large amounts of electricity and water and occupy land, and the state wants to control the pace and the terms. The approvals in MIDA's release are federal; the screening council is a separate gate at state level.

The timing of the release matters too. Nine-month figures land in November, when firms are setting next year's budgets and deciding where to place a plant, a lease or a service contract. The release is the reference document those decisions get checked against.

## So what

The gain lands on the firms that supply the projects. A data centre needs civil works, cooling systems, electrical fit-out, cabling, security and facilities management. An E&E plant needs precision machining, tooling, cleanroom services and logistics. The approvals tell a supplier where the work is concentrated: Johor for information and communications and E&E, Selangor and Pulau Pinang for manufacturing depth.

The cost lands on anyone who treats an approval as a purchase order. The mechanism is a delay between approval and spending. A project approved in the first nine months of 2026 is built over the following years, and the capital arrives in stages. A supplier who hires and buys equipment against an approval, before a contract is signed, carries the risk if the project slips.

The third actor is the state. Johor's 81.7% foreign share means the state's pipeline depends heavily on decisions made outside Malaysia — by United States firms, which supplied RM60.0 billion nationally, and by others. That is a strength while capital flows in and an exposure if it slows. The screening council is the state's tool for managing that exposure on the ground.

## FAQ

**Does an approved investment mean the money is already in Malaysia?**
No. An approval records that a project has been cleared to proceed at a stated capital value. The capital arrives over the years that follow, in stages tied to construction and commissioning. MIDA's release states the expected job creation "when fully implemented", which is itself a forward-looking phrase. A supplier or a jobseeker should treat the RM350.4 billion as a pipeline to be tracked, not as spending already booked. The release does not state how much of the total has been realised so far.

**Why does Johor's data-centre screening council matter to a small firm?**
Because it sits between a federal approval and a project a supplier can actually bid on. A data-centre proposal in Johor goes through state review on power, water and land use. Until a project clears that review, its construction schedule is not fixed, and a firm that hires or buys equipment against it carries the risk of a delay. Confirming which stage a project has reached is the practical step before committing resources.

**Which sectors should a Malaysian supplier watch in these numbers?**
Two stand out. Information and communications drove Johor's RM68.3 billion services figure, which points to data-centre fit-out, cooling, electrical works and facilities management. Electrical and electronics drove Johor's RM31.2 billion manufacturing figure and the 34.0% export growth, which points to precision machining, tooling and cleanroom services.
