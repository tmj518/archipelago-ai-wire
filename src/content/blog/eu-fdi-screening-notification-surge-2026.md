## What happened

The European Commission published its sixth annual report on the screening of foreign direct investment into the EU, and its trade department summarised the findings on 14 October 2026.

The headline movement is in volume. Notifications to the EU's cooperation mechanism increased by 40% between 2024 and 2025. In 2025, EU Member States notified 667 investments to the Commission, up from 477 notifications in 2024.

The second movement is in depth. Of the 667 cases notified, 88% were closed within 15 days. The other 12% of the investments notified to the Commission required an in-depth security risk assessment — a 4% rise compared to last year.

## Why now

Two things make this year's report more than a statistical update.

First, the composition of the caseload has shifted toward the sector where reviews bite. Most such cases were in manufacturing, which accounted for 53% of all transactions, up from 50% in 2024. A larger share of a larger pile of notifications is landing in the sector that generates in-depth assessments.

Second, the legal basis is about to change. The EU's new Foreign Investment Screening Regulation (FISR) was adopted on 17 June 2026 and will apply from 17 January 2028. The figures in this report describe the regime that is running now, not the one that will be running after that date.

## So what

For anyone notifying a deal, the practical shape of the process is now two-track, and the report gives the split.

> "Of the 667 cases notified, 88% were closed within 15 days."
> — European Commission, sixth annual FDI screening report, 14 October 2026

That sentence is the base case. The complement of it is the planning problem: 12% of notified investments went to in-depth assessment, and the report does not state how long those took. What it does state is where they concentrated — manufacturing, at 53% of all transactions.

## FAQ

**How many EU investments were notified in 2025?**

EU Member States notified 667 investments to the Commission in 2025, up from 477 notifications in 2024. That is the 40% increase the Commission reports between the two years. The count is of investments notified to the Commission through the EU cooperation mechanism, not of every foreign investment made in the EU — Member States screen nationally as well, and this figure covers the cases that reached the EU level. For an investor, the number to take away is the direction of travel: the population of deals that get looked at is growing, so the odds that yours is among them are rising rather than falling.

**What share of notified deals closed within 15 days?**

Of the 667 cases notified, 88% were closed within 15 days. That is the clear majority and it is the base case to plan a completion timetable around. The remaining 12% did not clear in that window: 12% of the investments notified to the Commission required an in-depth security risk assessment, a 4% rise compared to last year. The report does not state how long an in-depth assessment takes, so the 12% should be treated as an open-ended contingency rather than a fixed extension of the 15-day clock.

**Which sector carries the in-depth reviews?**

Manufacturing. Most such cases were in manufacturing, which accounted for 53% of all transactions, up from 50% in 2024. Two things are happening at once in that sentence: manufacturing is a larger share of the notified population than it was, and it is where the Commission says the in-depth cases concentrate. For a buyer of a manufacturing asset, the 12% in-depth rate is not a portfolio average to be spread across sectors — it is the rate that applies most directly to the deal in front of you.

**When does the new screening regulation apply?**

The EU's new Foreign Investment Screening Regulation (FISR) was adopted on 17 June 2026 and will apply from 17 January 2028. Until that date, notifications run under the cooperation mechanism whose 2025 results are described in this report. The date matters for transaction timing: a deal notified before 17 January 2028 and one notified after it are assessed under different rules, so a completion timetable that crosses that date should be reviewed rather than assumed to carry over unchanged.
