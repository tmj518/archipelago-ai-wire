## What happened

Two of NASA's four missions operating at Mars have suspended science operations after encountering separate technical issues in recent weeks, according to an Ars Technica article retrieved on 25 November 2026.

The first fault is on a rover. One of its two UHF radios was unresponsive when it powered up on October 30, triggering an automatic switch to the backup radio and placing the rover in safe mode. Ground teams at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California later discovered the backup UHF radio was transmitting at about half of its expected power output, NASA said in a statement the article cites.

The second fault is on a spacecraft that has lost the use of its wheels. Without three healthy wheels, engineers estimate the spacecraft has enough hydrazine fuel to last one to two months, NASA said.

The same article records a milestone that sits oddly beside the failures: the Perseverance rover surpassed the 28.06-mile (45.16-kilometer) mark set by NASA's Opportunity rover, which stopped working in 2018. The fleet is setting distance records and losing capabilities in the same month.

> **UHF radio:** a radio band used for short-range links. A Mars rover does not talk to Earth directly with it. The rover sends data to an orbiter passing overhead, and the orbiter relays it home. A weak UHF radio therefore does not silence the rover immediately; it slows the relay and forces the mission to ration what it sends.

The repair path is already contracted. In September 2026, NASA awarded Blue Origin a $700 million contract for the Mars Telecommunications Network, an orbiter dedicated to providing high-bandwidth connectivity between Earth and other missions operating on and around Mars. NASA wanted the orbiter completed by the end of 2028 and operational at Mars in 2030.

## Why now

The thread is age. Opportunity stopped working in 2018, and the distance record it left behind has only now been passed. The missions still flying are the survivors of a generation built to last roughly a decade, and the failures arriving in the same month are what the end of that design life looks like: a radio that will not power up, a wheel assembly that no longer gives three healthy units, a fuel tank with a countdown on it.

The second thread is the gap between the failure and the fix. The replacement communications orbiter was contracted in September 2026 and is wanted operational at Mars in 2030. Between the fuel running out in one to two months and a dedicated relay arriving in 2030 sits a stretch in which NASA has fewer working relays and less bandwidth for whatever is still operating.

The third thread is money and priority. A $700 million contract for a communications orbiter is a decision that Mars data is worth relaying even when the science instruments are failing. That is a statement about what NASA treats as infrastructure rather than as a mission.

## So what

The gain lands on Blue Origin, which holds a $700 million contract to build a relay that other missions depend on. A communications orbiter is a different commercial position from a science mission: it sells nothing to the public, but every other mission at Mars needs it, and the operator of the relay sits in the middle of the traffic.

The cost lands on the scientists who queued for time on the two suspended missions. A suspended science operation is not a paused experiment that resumes where it stopped. Instruments degrade, targets of opportunity pass, and the data that would have been collected in those weeks is simply not collected. That loss is not recoverable by fixing the radio later.

The third actor is the Deep Space Network, the ground antennas that talk to these spacecraft. Fewer healthy relays at Mars means the ground segment has to work harder to schedule what remains, and a mission that has to ration its downlink competes with every other mission for antenna time. The article does not give the ground-segment schedule, so the size of that squeeze is unknown.
