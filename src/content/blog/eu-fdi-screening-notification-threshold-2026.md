## What happened

The European Commission published its sixth annual report on the screening of foreign direct investment on 14 October 2026. The headline count is 667 investments notified to the Commission by EU Member States in 2025, against 477 notifications in 2024 — a 40% increase between the two years.

The processing split is the number that matters to a buyer. Of the 667 cases notified, 88% were closed within 15 days. The remaining 12% of notified investments required an in-depth security risk assessment, a share 4% higher than the year before. Most of those in-depth cases were in manufacturing, which accounted for 53% of all transactions, up from 50% in 2024.

Separately, the EU's new Foreign Investment Screening Regulation (FISR) was adopted on 17 June 2026 and applies from 17 January 2028. That is the instrument that will govern how these notifications are handled once it takes effect.

> **FDI screening notification** — the step a Member State takes when a foreign investor's planned acquisition or greenfield investment meets its national screening criteria. The Member State tells the Commission and the other Member States, who then have a window to raise security or public order concerns. A notification is not an approval and not a rejection; it is the start of a clock.

## Why now

Two clocks are running at once. The first is the notification volume: 667 cases in 2025 against 477 in 2024 is a 40% jump in a single year, and the in-depth share rose alongside it. More deals are being filed, and a larger slice of them are stopping for review.

The second is the instrument. FISR was adopted on 17 June 2026 and applies from 17 January 2028. A transaction that closes before that date runs under the current cooperation mechanism. A transaction structured to close after it runs under the new regulation. The gap between the two dates is the window in which a buyer chooses which regime their deal lands in.

## So what

The arithmetic a buyer needs is the probability of a stop, applied to the deal timetable.

Take 100 notified transactions at the 2025 rates. Eighty-eight close within 15 days. Twelve go to in-depth assessment. Of those twelve, roughly six sit in manufacturing, because manufacturing is 53% of transactions and the report places most in-depth cases there.

The cost is not a duty. It is the weeks a deal sits in review while the acquisition or the greenfield build waits. A buyer who plans a closing date without a review contingency is planning for the 88% case and ignoring the 12%.

Work it through on a mid-sized acquisition. A Chinese-funded buyer agrees to acquire a European manufacturing business. The purchase price is agreed, the financing is arranged, and the closing date is set. If the deal is notified and lands in the 12%, the closing slips by the length of an in-depth assessment. Every week of slippage carries the cost of the financing commitment, the cost of holding the target's operations steady, and the cost of the management time spent on the review rather than on integration.

Now run the same deal as a greenfield investment. The buyer is building a plant rather than buying one. The notification still applies if the Member State's screening criteria catch it. The 12% case here delays the start of construction, which pushes the first year of output. The cost is the revenue the plant would have earned in the slipped months, plus the carrying cost of the land and the permits held idle.

A third case: a buyer with several planned EU investments in the same year. At the 2025 rate, one in eight notifications stops. A portfolio of eight notified deals expects one in-depth review. A portfolio of sixteen expects two. The buyer who files them all against a single closing calendar absorbs the review time across the whole programme rather than one deal at a time.

The 53% manufacturing share sharpens this for anyone in goods. A Chinese-funded investment in a European factory is in the sector where most in-depth assessments land. A buyer in manufacturing should assume the 12% is not a background rate but the rate that applies to them.

The FISR date adds a fourth calculation. A deal that closes before 17 January 2028 runs under the current mechanism. A deal that closes after runs under the new regulation. A buyer who wants the current regime has a deadline to work back from; a buyer who wants the new one has a date to plan toward. Either way, the closing date is now a screening decision, not just a commercial one.
