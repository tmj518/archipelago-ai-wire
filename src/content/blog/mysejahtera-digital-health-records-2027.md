## AI Summary

The Health Ministry's Digital Health Division says more than 15 million people now have prescriptions and health records accessible in MySejahtera, the contact-tracing app that became a national health platform. Between 12 and 15 million people use it monthly. Fifty private hospitals and 3,000 GP clinics are connected to a certification network. The ministry aims to digitalise 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities by 2027, and all hospitals by 2029. For a household, the gain is a record that travels with you; the gap is that the clinic you visit next year is not yet on it.

## What happened

The Health Ministry's Digital Health Division has put numbers on what MySejahtera has become. Its director, Dr Maheshwara Rao Appannan, told Malay Mail that more than 15 million individuals now have their prescriptions and health records accessible inside the application. The app currently carries between 12 and 15 million monthly active users. At the onset of the pandemic, nearly 32 million people were onboarded.

The same interview describes the plumbing behind those records. The Malaysia Digital Health Certification Network currently connects more than 50 private hospitals and over 3,000 general practice clinics. A general practice clinic is the neighbourhood clinic a household visits for a cough, a fever or a repeat prescription.

The ministry also set out what is still to come. It aims to fully digitalise all 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities with electronic medical records by 2027. All hospitals nationwide are slated to be fully digitalised by 2029, a longer timeline that the ministry says reflects the more complex integration required in hospital settings.

Every figure in this article comes from that Malay Mail article, retrieved on 24 November 2026. The article does not publish the cost of the programme, the number of public hospitals already connected, the app's data-retention rules, or the process by which a patient corrects an error in a record. Those gaps are treated as gaps below.

> **Electronic medical record:** a patient's clinical history — prescriptions, lab results, diagnoses, dental notes — kept in a digital file rather than on paper. When two clinics hold the same record in the same format, a doctor at the second clinic can see what the first one prescribed. When they do not, the patient carries the history in their memory and a plastic bag of receipts.

## Why now

The thread starts with the pandemic. MySejahtera was built as a contact-tracing tool, and it onboarded nearly 32 million people because entering a shop required it. That is a reach no health ministry in the region bought with a marketing budget. The question since has been whether the reach converts into something a patient uses on an ordinary Tuesday.

The second thread is the certification network. Connecting 50 private hospitals and 3,000 GP clinics is the unglamorous half of digital health: agreeing a format, a security standard and a permission model so that one clinic's record can be read at another. That work has no launch event. It shows up as a number in an interview.

The third thread is the deadline. The 2027 target for 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities is roughly a year away from the retrieval date of this article. The 2029 hospital target is three years out. A ministry that publishes a dated target is inviting the public to check it, and that is why the numbers surfaced now rather than at a product launch.

## So what

The gain lands on the patient with a chronic condition. A person managing diabetes or high blood pressure visits a public clinic, a private GP and a specialist, and each one prescribes. When the records sit in one place, the second prescriber can see what the first one gave. The mechanism is duplication: without a shared record, the same test is run twice and the same drug is dispensed under two brand names.

The cost lands on the facilities that are not yet digitalised. A clinic that still keeps paper files cannot send a record into the network, so the patient's history is complete only up to the edge of the paper. The 2,918 and 670 figures are the size of that edge. Until 2027, the app's usefulness depends on which clinic the patient happens to walk into.

The third actor is the private sector. Fifty private hospitals and 3,000 GP clinics have chosen to connect. A private hospital that can pull a patient's public-clinic history saves its own staff time and avoids repeating tests, which is a commercial reason to join that has nothing to do with public spirit. The network grows because each participant gains from the others being on it.

## What it means for Malaysia

The Malaysian carrier is the patient at the counter, and the clinic staff who serve them. A household in Klang or Kota Bharu that visits a connected GP can expect the doctor to see a prescription history rather than ask for it. A household that visits a clinic still on paper gets the old experience. The difference is not the app on the phone; it is whether the building the patient walks into has been digitalised.

The second carrier is the dental visit. The 670 dental facilities in the 2027 target are where a filling, an extraction or a braces consultation gets recorded. Dental history is the part of a medical record people most often lose, because dental care is frequently paid for out of pocket and spread across providers. A record that follows the patient across clinics changes what a new dentist knows before the first X-ray.

The third carrier is the organ donor pledge. A digital pledge inside a health app is a record of intent that a transplant team can check, rather than a card in a wallet that is lost. The ministry's own framing places pledges inside the same platform as the records, which means the pledge inherits the app's login and its reach. ## How confident

**Confirmed.** The figures in this article come from a single named source: Dr Maheshwara Rao Appannan, director of the Health Ministry's Digital Health Division, quoted by Malay Mail and retrieved on 24 November 2026. The document states that more than 15 million individuals have prescriptions and health records accessible in the app, that monthly active users sit between 12 and 15 million, that 50 private hospitals and 3,000 GP clinics are connected, and that the ministry targets 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities by 2027 and all hospitals by 2029.

**Inferred.** The gap between 32 million onboarded and 12 to 15 million monthly active users is the measure of how many people keep the app installed but do not open it. That inference rests on the two figures being counted the same way, and the article does not say they are. The claim that a connected clinic can see a patient's prescription history follows from the network being described as a certification network, but the article does not describe the permission model, so a reader should treat the exact scope of what a doctor sees as unconfirmed.

**What would change my mind.** A published list of which facilities are already digitalised, a statement of the programme's cost, or a ministry document setting out what a patient does when a record is wrong. Any of those would move this from a description of a plan to a description of a service. A second independent source on the 15 million figure would also strengthen it; at present it rests on one interview.

## For you

- **A patient with a chronic condition:** open MySejahtera before your next appointment and check whether your prescription list matches what you are actually taking. If a clinic is not on the network, carry your last prescription slip; the app cannot show what was never entered.
- **A parent booking a dental visit:** ask at the counter whether the clinic records treatment electronically. The 670 dental facilities in the 2027 target are the ones that will, and a clinic that already does will have your child's history the next time you move house.
- **A household thinking about organ donation:** a digital pledge sits inside the same app as your records, so it is worth checking that your next of kin knows your decision. The pledge records intent; the conversation is what a transplant team will actually rely on.
- **A worker at a private clinic:** the 3,000 connected GP clinics are the ones whose staff now enter records in a format another clinic can read. That is a change to the daily routine, not a change to the job title.

## FAQ

**Is my health record already in MySejahtera?**

It depends on where you were treated. The Health Ministry says more than 15 million individuals have prescriptions and health records accessible in the app, and that 50 private hospitals and 3,000 GP clinics are connected to the Malaysia Digital Health Certification Network. If your usual clinic is one of those, the record is likely there. If it is a public primary care facility that has not yet been digitalised, the record is still on paper. The ministry aims to digitalise 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities by 2027, so the answer for any given clinic changes over the next year. Check the app before your appointment rather than assuming.

**Can a private doctor see what a public clinic prescribed?**

That is the purpose of the certification network, which connects private hospitals and GP clinics to a shared standard. The ministry's figures describe the network's reach, not the permission rules. The article does not state what a doctor sees, whether the patient approves each share, or how long a record stays visible. A patient should ask the doctor directly what appears on their screen. The practical test is simple: if the doctor reads back a prescription you did not mention, the record travelled; if they ask you to list your medications, it did not.

**What happens to the organ donor pledge I make in the app?**

The ministry places the pledge inside the same platform as the health records, which means it inherits the app's login and its reach. A digital pledge is a record of intent. It is not a substitute for telling your family. A transplant team works within the law and with the next of kin, so the conversation at home matters more than the tap in the app. Make the pledge, then tell the person who would be asked.

## The point

MySejahtera's second life is real but uneven. Fifteen million people can see their records, and 3,000 clinics can share them; 2,918 primary care facilities and 670 dental facilities are still waiting for the electronic record that makes sharing possible, with 2027 as the deadline and 2029 for hospitals. The app on the phone is the visible half. The clinic counter is the half that decides whether the record is there at all.
