## What happened

The US Army announced late on Thursday that it would delay a plan to blow up 68 deteriorating barrels of lead azide, a chemical used to detonate other explosives. The barrels are held at an Army installation in Middletown, Iowa. The detonation had been scheduled for Friday. It is now planned for Monday, 23 November 2026, to give the Army more time to communicate with Iowans.

The stockpile is not small. The lead azide in the 68 barrels totals around 9,700 pounds, or 4.4 tons. It sits suspended in a mixture of water and ethanol inside containers the report calls ECMs, and it has degraded and crystallised over time. That last detail is the reason the material is a problem rather than an asset: a crystallised detonator is unstable, and unstable explosives are destroyed rather than moved.

The modelling is the part that changed the timetable. Modelling released by the Army on Wednesday, based on the conditions forecast for the Friday detonation, showed the plume drifting up to 100 miles north of the installation. Airborne lead at a level of 0.18 micrograms per cubic metre would reach nearly 100 miles north, toward Cedar Rapids, Iowa. The US Environmental Protection Agency's standard for lead in the air is 0.15 micrograms per cubic metre. The same modelling suggested lead dust would deposit at between 5 and 100 micrograms per square foot in a stretch up to 25 miles north, nearly to Wapello, Iowa.

Every figure in this article comes from the Ars Technica article retrieved on 20 November 2026. The Army has not published the full modelling, the population counts inside the plume path, or the disposal method it will use if open detonation is ruled out.

> **Open detonation:** destroying unstable explosive material by setting it off in the open air rather than inside a sealed chamber. It is fast and cheap, and it releases the material's contents — here, lead — into the air as a plume that travels with the wind and settles as dust. A sealed chamber, by contrast, burns the material and captures the exhaust.

The gap between the modelled level and the standard is the whole story, and it is small enough to set out plainly.

## Why now

The thread runs in three steps, and the third one is this week. First, the Army accumulated a stockpile of a deteriorating detonator it no longer wants: 68 barrels, 9,700 pounds, crystallising in a water-and-ethanol mix. Second, it chose open detonation as the disposal route and set a date. Third, it ran the plume model for that date and published the result on Wednesday.

The model is what forced the delay. A detonation on Friday would have put airborne lead at 0.18 micrograms per cubic metre across a path reaching nearly 100 miles north — above the 0.15 standard, not far above it, but above. Publishing that number and proceeding two days later is a different act from publishing it and pausing. The Army paused.

The stated reason for the pause is communication, not the model. The Army said it would wait until Monday, 23 November, to provide more time to communicate about the situation with Iowans. That is a statement about process. It tells the reader what the Army says it is doing with the extra days; it does not say the detonation has been cancelled, and it does not say the method has changed.

## So what

The gain lands on the residents inside the modelled path, who get three more days of information before a decision that affects the air they breathe. Whether three days is enough to change anything depends on what the Army says in them, and that is not yet on the record.

The cost lands on the Army's own disposal schedule. A stockpile of crystallised lead azide does not become safer by waiting. Every day the barrels sit is a day the material remains unstable, and the reason it is being destroyed is that instability. The delay trades a short-term air-quality risk for a slightly longer period of storage risk. That trade is the Army's to make, and it has made it without publishing the storage-risk assessment.

The third actor is the credibility of the modelling itself. A model that produces 0.18 against a standard of 0.15 is a model operating close to its own uncertainty. The article reports the figure; it does not report a confidence interval, and the Army has not published one. A reader should treat 0.18 as the Army's central estimate, not as a measurement.
