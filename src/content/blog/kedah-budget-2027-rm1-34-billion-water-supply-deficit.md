## What happened

The Kedah state government presented Belanjawan 2027, its budget for next year, on 15 November 2026. FMT and Bernama reported the figures the same day. The budget totals RM1.34 billion.

The document sets out four numbers that matter to a household in the state. Estimated revenue for 2027 is RM850 million. Operating expenditure — in Malay, Perbelanjaan Mengurus, the money spent on running the state day to day, such as salaries and services — is RM940 million. Development spending, the money for building things, is RM401.28 million. And the state projects an operating deficit of RM90 million, which it expresses as 10.59% of something in the budget.

Within the development total sits the Kumpulan Wang Pembangunan Bekalan Air, the water supply development fund. It receives RM143.75 million. Of that, RM138.75 million comes from a federal government loan to carry out three loan projects, and RM5 million comes from the state's own allocation.

> **Operating deficit (defisit mengurus):** the gap between what a state collects in a year and what it spends running itself — salaries, services, upkeep — before any building projects are counted. Kedah reports its 2027 gap as RM90 million, or 10.59% of the budget base the state uses for the ratio.

The revenue side is concentrated. Land revenue is the largest component at RM551 million. Various revenue follows at RM253 million, forest revenue at RM30 million and district revenue at RM16 million.

## Why now

The water numbers are the visible end of a thread that runs back years. Kedah has carried a structural gap between what it collects and what it spends on itself. The state's own figures show the operating deficit ratio at 12.65% in 2025, then 11.81% in 2026, and now a target of 10.59% for 2027. That is a planned three-year slide in the same direction, and the 2027 budget is the third step in it.

The second thread is how the state pays for water infrastructure. The budget does not fund the three water projects from revenue. It funds them from a federal loan, with a small state top-up. That is a choice about who carries the cost and when: the projects get built in 2027, but the repayment obligation sits on the state's books beyond it.

The third thread is the revenue base. Land revenue at RM551 million is the largest single source in a RM850 million revenue estimate. A revenue plan that leans on land is sensitive to how much land changes hands and how it is valued in a given year. The budget document, as reported, does not break down what happens to the ratio if land revenue comes in lower.

## So what

The gain lands on households and businesses in areas served by the three water projects. A water supply project that goes ahead changes the reliability of taps and the frequency of disruptions. The mechanism is direct: the loan converts a project the state can not fund from its own revenue into construction that starts in 2027.

The cost lands on the state's future budgets. A loan is repaid from future revenue, which means money that would otherwise go to services or other projects is committed. The state has not, in the reported figures, said what the repayment schedule looks like. What the document does say is that the state expects its operating deficit ratio to fall each year; a loan repayment sits outside the operating deficit as the budget defines it, so the two numbers do not cancel each other out.

The third actor is the federal government. Putrajaya is lending RM138.75 million to a state that is running an operating deficit. That is a transfer of capacity from the centre to the state, and it ties the two governments together for the life of the loan. The incentive for the state is to get infrastructure built without raising revenue; the incentive for the federation is to keep a member state's basic services working.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on Kedah water consumers and on the contractors and workers who build the three projects. A household in an area covered by the works faces the disruption of construction and, later, the benefit of a more reliable supply. A small contractor bidding for the work faces a pipeline that exists because a federal loan was approved, not because state revenue grew.

The concrete carrier is the water bill and the tap. Water supply in Peninsular Malaysia is a state matter, and the cost of building it eventually shows up in the tariff structure and in the service a household receives. The 2027 budget does not state a tariff change, and this article does not claim one. What it states is that RM138.75 million of borrowed money is going into three water projects next year.

The second carrier is the state's own payroll and services. If the operating deficit ratio falls as targeted, the state is squeezing its running costs relative to its revenue base. That squeeze reaches the people the state employs and the services it provides. The budget as reported does not say where the squeeze falls.

## FAQ

That works out to about 96.5% borrowed and 3.5% state money, using the figures reported by FMT and Bernama on 15 November 2026. The loan covers three projects. The budget report does not state the repayment terms, the interest rate or the schedule, so the cost of the borrowing beyond 2027 is not in the document as reported.

**What is the operating deficit and why does the ratio matter?**

The operating deficit is the gap between what the state collects and what it spends running itself — salaries, services and upkeep — before building projects are counted. Kedah reports a RM90 million gap for 2027, expressed as 10.59% of the budget base the state uses. The ratio matters because it shows direction: 12.65% in 2025, 11.81% in 2026 and a 10.59% target for 2027. A falling ratio means the state plans to close the gap gradually rather than in one year. The report does not say which spending is restrained to achieve it.

**Where does Kedah's money come from?**

Land revenue is the largest single source in the 2027 revenue estimate at RM551 million, out of RM850 million expected in total. Various revenue contributes RM253 million, forest revenue RM30 million and district revenue RM16 million. The concentration in land matters because land revenue depends on transactions and valuations in a given year. The budget report, as covered, does not include a sensitivity showing what happens to the deficit ratio if land revenue comes in below RM551 million, so the resilience of the plan to a slower land market is not stated.
