Six court cases have been decided, with fines totalling RM19,500. The Risk Mitigation Code took effect on 1 June 2026.

## What happened

The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC), the regulator that oversees communications and online content in Malaysia, published a set of enforcement figures on AI misuse. Malay Mail reported them on 17 November 2026.

The headline number is 13,883. That is the count of deepfake items that platform providers removed between 1 January 2024 and 31 October 2026, after takedown requests from the MCMC based on public complaints. Of those, 1,178 items involved political leaders.

A second set of figures covers enforcement under the law. Between 1 January 2025 and 31 October 2026, the MCMC investigated 61 cases involving AI misuse under Section 233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588). Of those, 10 cases went to court. Compounds totalling RM755,000 were issued in seven cases, and 17 warning letters were served. Six of the 10 court cases have been decided, with fines totalling RM19,500.

> **Deepfake:** an image, video or audio recording that has been generated or altered with artificial intelligence so that a person appears to say or do something they did not. The output looks real enough to be shared as evidence, which is what makes it useful to a scammer and damaging to the person depicted.

The two windows do not match, and that matters for reading the numbers. The takedown count runs from 1 January 2024. The enforcement count runs from 1 January 2025. They describe different things: one is content removed at the regulator's request, the other is cases pursued against people.

## Why now

The thread here is a shift in who carries the burden of cleaning up harmful content. For most of the past two years, the visible mechanism was the takedown request: the regulator asks, the platform removes. That is what produced the 13,883 figure, and it depends on a complaint arriving first.

The second thread is the licensing rule. Under the Risk Mitigation Code, which took effect on 1 June 2026, licensed service providers are required to establish mechanisms to identify, assess and address harmful content. That moves the obligation upstream. A platform no longer waits for a request; it is required to have a system that finds and handles the material.

The third thread is enforcement against people rather than posts. The 61 investigations, 10 court cases, RM755,000 in compounds and 17 warning letters describe a regulator building a record of cases under Section 233, which is the provision in the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 that deals with improper use of network facilities. The figures surfaced this month because the commission laid them out together.

## So what

The gain lands on the person whose face or voice has been copied. A deepfake of a private individual used to spread a scam is removed faster when a platform has a standing duty to look for it, rather than waiting for the victim to find it, report it, and wait. The mechanism is a shift in who starts the process.

The cost lands on platforms. A duty to identify, assess and address harmful content requires staff, tooling and a paper trail. That cost is real, and it is borne by every licensed provider operating in Malaysia, not only the large ones. A small platform with a Malaysian user base faces the same obligation as a global one.

The third actor is the person who makes and shares the deepfake. The enforcement figures show the regulator's reach is narrow in volume terms: 61 investigations and 10 court cases over 22 months, against 13,883 items removed. The removal channel is wide and fast; the prosecution channel is narrow and slow. That is the shape of the system, and it tells a reader where the risk sits. A creator of a deepfake faces a real chance of losing the post and a smaller chance of facing a case.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on three groups a Malaysian reader will recognise.

The first is the ordinary user who receives a deepfake in a WhatsApp group or sees one on a social feed. The 13,883 figure exists because someone complained. The takedown system runs on public reports, so a reader who spots a fake video of a public figure or a private person and reports it is feeding the only channel that produced that number. The related piece on [scam post removal requests reaching 174,099](/blog/mcmc-scam-post-removal-requests-174099-october-2026/) shows the same complaint-driven machinery at work on a larger volume of scam content.

The second is the small business owner whose face is used in a fake endorsement. A deepfake video of a shop owner recommending an investment product is a fraud aimed at that owner's own customers. The removal route is the platform, prompted by an MCMC request. The enforcement route is Section 233, and the figures show it is used sparingly.

The third is the platform operator with Malaysian users. Since 1 June 2026, the Risk Mitigation Code requires a mechanism to identify, assess and address harmful content. That is a procedure a Malaysian firm must be able to describe if asked. The regional context sits in [Malaysia's push for an Asean cybersecurity strategy](/blog/asean-cybersecurity-cooperation-strategy-2026-2030-malaysia/), where online scams are the stated driver.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The figures come from MCMC statements reported by Malay Mail on 17 November 2026: 13,883 deepfake items removed between 1 January 2024 and 31 October 2026, of which 1,178 involved political leaders; 61 AI-misuse cases investigated under Section 233 between 1 January 2025 and 31 October 2026; 10 brought to court; RM755,000 in compounds across seven cases; 17 warning letters; six court cases decided with fines totalling RM19,500. The Risk Mitigation Code took effect on 1 June 2026 and requires licensed service providers to establish mechanisms to identify, assess and address harmful content.

**Inferred.** The gap between 13,883 removals and 61 investigations is consistent with a system that resolves most complaints at the platform level and pursues a small number as cases. That reading is mine, not the commission's. The same applies to the reading that the Risk Mitigation Code shifts work from the complaint desk to the platform's own systems: the document states the obligation, and the effect on workload is an inference.

**What would change my mind.** A published breakdown of the 13,883 items by platform, by type of harm, or by whether the person depicted was a public figure or a private individual would sharpen every conclusion here. So would a figure for how many complaints the commission received, as distinct from how many items were removed. Without those, the removal count describes output, not the size of the problem.

## How we calculated this

The figures allow one honest comparison: the average compound per case, and the average fine per decided court case. Both use only numbers from the MCMC statement.

```text
Compounds: RM755,000 across 7 cases
  RM755,000 / 7 = RM107,857.14 per case (rounded to the sen)

Court fines: RM19,500 across 6 decided cases
  RM19,500 / 6 = RM3,250.00 per decided case

Ratio of average compound to average court fine:
  RM107,857.14 / RM3,250.00 = 33.19 times
```

The arithmetic shows the two routes are priced very differently. A compound resolves a case at an average of about RM107,857; a decided court case produces an average fine of RM3,250. The comparison is between two different legal mechanisms, not two prices for the same outcome, and the commission did not explain how it chooses between them.

## For you

- **A small business owner whose face appears in a fake endorsement:** report the post to the platform and keep a record of the link and the date, because the takedown route described here runs on complaints reaching the regulator.
- **A student or job seeker sent a video message from someone claiming to be an employer:** treat a request for payment or personal documents in a video as a warning sign, and verify through a second channel the sender did not control.

## FAQ

**What is a deepfake and why does the MCMC count them?**

A deepfake is an image, video or audio recording generated or altered with artificial intelligence so a person appears to say or do something they did not. The MCMC counts them because they are used to defraud people and to damage reputations, and because platforms will remove them when the regulator asks. Between 1 January 2024 and 31 October 2026, platforms removed 13,883 such items following MCMC takedown requests based on public complaints. Of those, 1,178 involved political leaders. The count measures removals, not the number of fakes created or shared.

**What happens to a person who makes or shares a deepfake in Malaysia?**

The MCMC investigated 61 cases involving AI misuse under Section 233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 between 1 January 2025 and 31 October 2026. Ten cases went to court. Compounds totalling RM755,000 were issued in seven cases, and 17 warning letters were served. Six of the 10 court cases have been decided, with fines totalling RM19,500. The figures describe outcomes the commission has published; they do not set out how it decides between a compound, a warning and a court case.

**What must platforms operating in Malaysia do about harmful content?**

Under the Risk Mitigation Code, which took effect on 1 June 2026, licensed service providers are required to establish mechanisms to identify, assess and address harmful content. That is an obligation to have a working process, not only to respond when a complaint arrives. The code sits alongside the takedown route that produced the 13,883 removals. A reader who wants the wider regional picture can start with [the Asean cybersecurity strategy piece](/blog/asean-cybersecurity-cooperation-strategy-2026-2030-malaysia/), and a reader tracking platform obligations can follow [the digital services tax amendment](/blog/service-tax-digital-services-amendment-regulations-2026/).

## The point

The number that matters is not 13,883. It is the shape around it: a wide, complaint-driven removal channel, a narrow prosecution channel, and a licensing rule that since 1 June 2026 puts the duty to look for harmful content on the platform rather than on the person who was copied. For a Malaysian reader, that means the fastest remedy is still a report, and the durable change is a procedure a platform now has to be able to show.
