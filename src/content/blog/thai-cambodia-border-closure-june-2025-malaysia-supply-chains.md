## What happened

Thailand closed every border checkpoint with Cambodia starting in June 2025, and the closure has held. That is the central fact in ISEAS Perspective 2026/77, "Nationalism and Militarisation in Thai-Cambodian Border Conflict 2025-26", written by Puangthong Pawakapan and published by the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute on 29 October 2026.

The paper records a second date that shows how the closure has become an international problem rather than a bilateral quarrel. On 10 September 2026, caretaker Defence Minister General Natthapol Nakpanich announced that a foreign government had formally requested both Thailand and Cambodia to reopen specific border crossings in Chanthaburi and Trat, two Thai provinces on the Cambodian frontier. The paper does not name the government that made the request.

The cost inside Thailand is stated in the same document. The eastern region's agricultural sector relies on as many as 100,000 Cambodian workers, and a closed border cuts that labour supply. The paper also sets a benchmark for how serious the rupture is: it marks a profound fracture in relations not seen since Cambodia joined ASEAN in 1999.

> **Border checkpoint:** a designated crossing point where a country's immigration and customs officers inspect people and goods entering or leaving. When every checkpoint on a frontier closes, legal trade and legal labour movement across that frontier stop, and goods and workers either stop moving or move through informal routes.

## Why now

The closure is more than a year old, so the news this month is not that it began. The news is that it has outlasted the usual life of a border dispute and has begun to draw in governments outside the two countries.

The thread runs through Thai domestic politics. On 29 August 2025 the Thai Constitutional Court dismissed Prime Minister Paetongtarn from office over ethical violations, as the paper records. A caretaker government then held the border file, and it is a caretaker defence minister, General Natthapol Nakpanich, who announced the foreign request on 10 September 2026. A caretaker administration has less room to make concessions than a settled one, which is one reason a closure that began as a pressure tactic has stayed shut.

The second thread is that the dispute has widened beyond the border itself. The paper records that the Anutin administration unilaterally abrogated the 2001 Memorandum of Understanding on overlapping Gulf of Thailand claims. A memorandum of understanding is a written but non-binding agreement between governments. Abrogating one removes a channel for talking, and it tells the other side that the dispute now covers maritime territory as well as land crossings.

The third thread is the request from outside. A foreign government asking two neighbours to reopen named crossings is a signal that the closure is damaging interests beyond Thailand and Cambodia. The paper reports the request and the minister's announcement. It does not report an answer, and it does not report that any crossing has reopened.

## So what

The judgement is that a political dispute between two neighbours can hold a regional supply chain shut for more than a year, and that the reopening decision sits with governments rather than with the firms that need the route.

The mechanism is a loop with a delay. A closure raises the cost of moving goods and labour across the frontier. Higher costs hurt the businesses and farms that depend on the crossing. Those businesses press their governments to reopen. But the same closure is also a political instrument, and a government that reopens under pressure appears to have lost the dispute. So the pressure to reopen and the pressure to hold firm build at the same time, and the loop settles into a standoff rather than resolving. The delay is the year and more that has passed since June 2025.

Who pays first is the group with the least ability to route around the closure. In Thailand's east, that is the agricultural sector that relies on as many as 100,000 Cambodian workers. A farm cannot move its harvest to a different border the way a factory can move a shipment. Labour-intensive work absorbs the shock first, because the workers simply do not arrive.

Who gains is the operator who can route around a closed frontier. A logistics firm that already runs goods through a third country, or a buyer who has a second supplier outside the affected corridor, keeps trading while a competitor who built a single route waits. That advantage is a position, not a guarantee: the same firm is exposed if the next closure lands on its alternative route.

What changes is the price of assuming a border stays open. A firm that treated a land crossing as a permanent feature of its cost base now has to treat it as a variable that a government sets. That is a change in how a supply chain is planned, not only in what it costs this year.
