<h1>Danny Postma's HeadshotPro: A One-Person AI Headshot Business</h1>

<p>Danny Postma runs HeadshotPro, a service that turns ordinary selfies into professional-looking business headshots using AI, and he has publicly described it as a one-person company based in the Netherlands. The business model is simple: a customer uploads photos, pays a flat fee, and receives a batch of generated headshots within a stated turnaround. What makes the case worth studying is not the AI itself — it is that a single operator built a productized service around a narrow, high-intent search need ("professional headshot") and priced it against the alternative of a studio photographer. This article covers who he is, what the product does, how customers find it, how it makes money, and which parts a solo operator can realistically copy. Figures below are attributed to the sources listed at the end; where a number could not be confirmed from a primary source, it is not stated.</p>

<h2>Who the founder is</h2>

<p>Danny Postma is a Dutch indie developer and solo founder. He is best known in the bootstrapper community for building and shipping small software products on his own, and he documents his work on his personal site, <a href="https://dannypostma.com/">dannypostma.com</a>. HeadshotPro is the product most associated with him: an AI headshot generator marketed to professionals, teams and job seekers who need a credible portrait without booking a photographer.</p>

<p>Two things about his positioning matter for anyone copying the model. First, he operates as a solo founder rather than a funded team, which means the product had to be simple enough to run without a support department. Second, he built in a category where the buyer already knows what they want and is already searching for it — "AI headshot," "professional headshot generator," "LinkedIn photo" — rather than having to be educated into a new behaviour.</p>

<h2>What the product is</h2>

<p>HeadshotPro is a web application. A customer uploads a set of photos of themselves, the system generates professional-style headshots, and the customer downloads the ones they like. The product page at <a href="https://www.headshotpro.com/">headshotpro.com</a> presents it as a headshot service for individuals and for teams, with tiers that differ by how many people are covered and how many photos are delivered.</p>

<p>The important design choice is that it is sold as an outcome, not as a tool. The customer is not buying credits or a model; they are buying "a professional headshot." That framing lets the product be priced against photographers rather than against other AI tools, and it lets the marketing speak in the customer's language instead of in technical terms.</p>

<h2>How customers find it</h2>

<p>HeadshotPro's acquisition is built on search intent and on the founder's public presence, rather than on paid scale.</p>

<p><strong>Search-driven landing pages.</strong> The product sits on a domain that names the category directly, and the site is structured around the query a buyer would type. This is the classic productized-service playbook: own the generic phrase, then convert the visitor with a clear before/after and a price.</p>

<p><strong>Founder distribution.</strong> Postma has an audience among indie hackers and solo founders, and he ships in public. That audience does not buy headshots at volume, but it produces the thing that actually matters for a solo operator: links, mentions and word of mouth that compound without ad spend. Public discussion of his products on communities such as Hacker News and Indie Hackers is part of how the name travels.</p>

<p><strong>Team and B2B upsell.</strong> Because the product page addresses teams as well as individuals, a single satisfied customer can turn into a company order. For a one-person business, that is the highest-leverage acquisition channel available: one conversation, many seats.</p>

<p><strong>Comparison and alternative pages.</strong> Category-defining products in a new space attract searches like "HeadshotPro vs [photographer]" or "AI headshot alternatives." Owning those pages is a standard, low-cost way for a solo operator to capture buyers who are already close to a decision.</p>

<h2>Business model and pricing</h2>

<p>The model is a one-off purchase per customer (or per team), not a subscription. That is a deliberate fit for a solo operator: no churn dashboard, no recurring billing support load, and revenue that arrives at the moment of intent. Pricing is tiered by volume and by whether the buyer is an individual or a team; exact current prices change, so check the official pricing page at <a href="https://www.headshotpro.com/">headshotpro.com</a> rather than relying on any figure quoted second-hand.</p>

<h2>Startup cost and scale</h2>

<p>The startup cost profile of this kind of business is unusually low for a service business: a domain, hosting, payment processing, and pay-per-use inference costs for image generation. There is no studio, no photographer to schedule, and no inventory. The real cost is the founder's time on the generation pipeline and on support.</p>

<p>On scale, Postma has publicly characterised HeadshotPro as a one-person business, and it has been widely discussed in the indie-founder community as one of the larger solo AI products. Specific annual revenue figures circulate in interviews and social posts; because those numbers move and are sometimes quoted out of context, this article does not restate one. If you need a figure for a decision, take it from his own site or a dated interview and cite the date alongside it.</p>

<h2>What a solo operator can copy</h2>

<p><strong>1. Sell the outcome, not the technology.</strong> The customer wants a headshot, not a diffusion model. Naming the product after the result ("HeadshotPro") makes the value obvious in a search result and removes the explanation step.</p>

<p><strong>2. Pick a category with existing search demand.</strong> "Professional headshot" is a phrase people already type. Building on existing intent is far cheaper than creating a new category, especially with no ad budget.</p>

<p><strong>3. Price against the incumbent, not against other AI tools.</strong> The comparison the buyer makes is a photographer's session fee, not a competitor's credit pack. That comparison supports a higher one-off price and avoids a race to the bottom.</p>

<p><strong>4. Use one-off pricing if you are alone.</strong> A single purchase per customer removes churn management, dunning and cancellation support — three things that quietly consume a solo founder's week.</p>

<p><strong>5. Build a team tier early.</strong> The same product sold to a company is one sale with many seats. For a one-person business, B2B orders are the difference between a side project and a business.</p>

<p><strong>6. Ship in public to earn distribution.</strong> Postma's audience among indie founders produced links and mentions that a solo operator cannot buy at small scale. Consistent public building is a marketing channel, not vanity.</p>

<p><strong>7. Keep the operation boring.</strong> A solo business survives on low operational surface area: few moving parts, clear turnaround promises, and a support load one person can answer.</p>

<h2>What doesn't work</h2>

<p><strong>Competing on model quality alone.</strong> Image generation improves across the whole market every few months. A solo operator who positions on "our AI is better" is competing with every well-funded lab and will be overtaken. Positioning on the outcome and the customer segment survives model upgrades; positioning on the model does not.</p>

<p><strong>Subscriptions for a one-time need.</strong> Most people need a headshot once every few years. Forcing a subscription onto a one-off need creates churn, refunds and support tickets that a single founder cannot absorb.</p>

<p><strong>Ignoring the refund and redo problem.</strong> AI output is inconsistent. If the product does not handle "these don't look like me" gracefully — with a redo or a refund policy — the support inbox becomes the business. Public discussions of AI headshot services repeatedly raise likeness and quality complaints; plan for them before launch, not after.</p>

<p><strong>Buying ads before the page converts.</strong> Paid traffic on a weak landing page is the fastest way for a solo founder to burn the entire budget. The category leaders in this space built organic intent pages first.</p>

<p><strong>Promising turnaround you cannot meet alone.</strong> If the stated delivery window depends on one person being awake, a single bad week becomes a public trust problem.</p>
