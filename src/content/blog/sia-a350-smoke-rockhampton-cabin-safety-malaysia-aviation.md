## So what

The judgement is this: a plane can be cleared for service and still turn back. That is not a contradiction. Airworthiness clearance means the aircraft met its maintenance and inspection requirements at the time of release. A cabin smoke event is an in-flight condition, and the response to it is a crew procedure, not a verdict on the maintenance record.

The gain lands on the safety system itself. Two events in a week on one airframe generate data: sensor readings, crew reports, inspection findings. That data feeds back into maintenance schedules and component checks. The mechanism is a feedback loop — an event produces a report, the report changes a procedure or a check interval, and the next aircraft benefits. The delay in that loop is the time between the event and the publication of the finding.

The cost lands on the operator and, indirectly, on the maintenance organisations that support the fleet. A second event triggers deeper inspection, which takes the aircraft out of service, which costs flying hours. The party that absorbs the cost first is the operator; the party that absorbs the workload is the maintenance organisation.

What changes is scrutiny. Cabin smoke detection systems and the procedures around them move up the inspection agenda for the A350-900 fleet. That is an inference from the sequence of events, not a statement from SIA or from any regulator.
