The Global Anti-Scam Alliance's State of Scams in Malaysia 2026 report, reported by Malay Mail on 28 November 2026, found that 83 per cent of Malaysian adults met a scam this year, and that 70 per cent of those who met one still interacted with it. The gap between knowing about scams and not engaging with them is the finding that matters for a household.

## What happened

The Global Anti-Scam Alliance, a research body that surveys scam exposure across countries, published its State of Scams in Malaysia 2026 report. Malay Mail reported the findings on 28 November 2026. Every figure in this article comes from that report as reported by Malay Mail, retrieved on 28 November 2026.

The headline finding is a gap. 83 per cent of Malaysian adults encountered a scam in 2026. Of those who encountered one, 70 per cent still interacted with it. That is seven in 10 people who met a scam attempt and did not simply put the phone down or delete the message.

The report also records where the encounters happened. Phone calls were the most common channel at 69 per cent. WhatsApp was cited by 67 per cent of respondents and Telegram by 40 per cent. One in seven Malaysian parents, 14 per cent, said their children aged seven to 17 had experienced at least one scam.

On the remedy side, 45 per cent of Malaysian adults believe banks should always reimburse scam victims, and 78 per cent support banks blocking transactions above a certain value when they suspect a scam link.

> **The confidence gap:** the distance between a person's belief that they can recognise a scam and their behaviour when one arrives. A person who has read the warnings, knows the tricks and still stays on the call to "check" is inside the gap. The report measures the behaviour, not the belief: it counts who interacted, not who felt confident.

## Why now

The thread is the shift in how scams arrive. Phone calls at 69 per cent and WhatsApp at 67 per cent describe a scam economy that runs on the two tools a Malaysian household uses every day: the voice call and the family group chat. A scam no longer needs a fake website or an email that lands in a spam folder. It needs a number that looks local and a message that looks like it came from someone the reader knows.

The second thread is the awareness campaign itself. Public awareness campaigns are the most cited source of prevention education in the report's findings, which is why the confidence gap matters now: the campaigns have done their job of making people aware, and the behaviour has not followed at the same speed. Awareness and behaviour are two different measurements, and the report puts a number on the distance between them.

The third thread is the reimbursement debate. 45 per cent of adults believe banks should always reimburse victims, while 78 per cent support banks blocking suspicious transactions above a certain value. Those two positions pull in opposite directions: a reader who wants the bank to stop the payment and also wants the bank to pay it back is asking the bank to carry the risk of the customer's own decision. That tension is live, and the report records it rather than resolving it.

## So what

The gain lands on the scam operator. A 70 per cent engagement rate against a 83 per cent encounter rate means the operator's cost of reaching a live target is low and the conversion funnel is wide. The operator does not need to defeat an aware person's judgement; the operator needs the person to stay on the line for one more minute, which is a much smaller ask.

The cost lands on the household that engages. The report does not publish the size of losses, so this article does not state one. What it does state is the exposure: eight in 10 adults met a scam this year, and seven in 10 of those stayed in the conversation. The loss, when it comes, is borne by the person who transferred the money, not by the platform that carried the message or the bank that processed the payment.

The third actor is the bank. The public wants two things at once: blocking at 78 per cent and reimbursement at 45 per cent. A bank that blocks a transaction protects the customer's money and takes a complaint; a bank that reimburses every victim socialises the loss across all customers. The report records the public's preference on both, and the two preferences do not fit together without a rule that decides who pays.

## What it means for Malaysia

The Malaysian carrier is the household phone. A call at 69 per cent and a WhatsApp message at 67 per cent are not exotic attack surfaces; they are the two channels a family already trusts. The concrete consequence is that the moment of decision arrives inside a conversation the reader is already having, and the report's 70 per cent says most readers stay in that conversation.

The second carrier is the parent. 14 per cent of parents said a child aged seven to 17 experienced a scam. A child on a phone in a household where the adults are also being targeted is a second entry point, and the report puts a number on how often that entry point was used.

The third carrier is the bank branch and the banking app. With 78 per cent support for blocking suspicious transactions, a reader should expect more friction on a large or unusual transfer: a hold, a call-back, a branch visit. That friction is the policy the public says it wants, and it lands on the customer at the counter. ## How we calculated this

The report gives two shares that describe the same population from different angles: the share of adults who met a scam, and the share of those who met one and still engaged. The second is a share of the first, not of all adults. The arithmetic below converts the engagement rate into a share of all Malaysian adults, using only the two figures the report supplies.

```text
Adults who encountered a scam            = 83 per cent
Of those, share who still interacted     = 70 per cent

Share of all adults who met a scam
and still interacted                     = 83 per cent x 70 per cent
                                         = 0.83 x 0.70
                                         = 0.581
                                         = 58.1 per cent

Check: adults who met a scam and did not interact
                                         = 83 per cent - 58.1 per cent
                                         = 24.9 per cent
```

Read plainly: on the report's own two figures, roughly 58 in every 100 Malaysian adults met a scam in 2026 and stayed in the conversation. The calculation assumes the 70 per cent is measured against the same group as the 83 per cent, which is how the report presents it. The report does not publish the survey sample size, so the margin of error on these shares is not stated here.

## FAQ

**Does the report say Malaysians are overconfident about spotting scams?**
The report measures behaviour, not confidence. It states that 83 per cent of Malaysian adults encountered a scam in 2026 and that 70 per cent of those who encountered one still interacted with it. The word "confidence" is a description of the gap between awareness and action, not a survey question the report publishes. What the report does support is that awareness of scams is widespread, because public awareness campaigns are the most cited source of prevention education in its findings, and that engagement remains high despite that awareness. A reader should treat the confidence framing as an interpretation of the two shares, not as a separate finding with its own number.

**Which channel should a Malaysian household watch most closely?**
On the report's figures, the phone call is the most common channel at 69 per cent of encounters, with WhatsApp close behind at 67 per cent and Telegram at 40 per cent. The three overlap, because a single scam can start with a call and continue in a chat. The practical reading is that the household's own phone is the front line: an unexpected call about an account, a delivery or an official matter, and a message that arrives from a familiar name but asks for something unusual. The report does not publish a loss figure by channel, so it does not rank the channels by how much money is taken through each.

**What does the report say about who should bear the loss?**
It records two public preferences that pull against each other. 45 per cent of Malaysian adults believe banks should always reimburse scam victims, and 78 per cent support banks blocking suspicious transactions above a certain value when a scam link is suspected. A bank that blocks a payment protects the customer's money and invites a complaint; a bank that reimburses every victim spreads the loss across all its customers. The report states the preferences and does not resolve them. For a reader, the practical consequence is more friction on an unusual transfer, and the expectation of a hold or a call-back should be treated as the policy the public says it wants.

## The point

The number to carry away is not 83 per cent. It is 70 per cent: the share of people who met a scam and stayed in the conversation anyway. Awareness campaigns have made the warning familiar, and familiarity is not the same as a habit. The habit that closes the gap is small and boring: end the call, close the chat, ring back on a number you found yourself.
