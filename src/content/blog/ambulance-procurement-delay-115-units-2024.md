## What happened

The Health Ministry told the Dewan Rakyat that it was supposed to receive 590 ambulances by the end of 2024. The company holding the contract supplied 115 units across the whole contract period, which ended in November 2024. The remaining 475 vehicles never arrived.

The ministry also told the House that 989 ambulances are now caught in a procurement delay. Against that gap, Putrajaya has allocated funds under Budget 2027 to buy another 400 ambulances, which the ministry says brings the total number to 1,389.

The statement was made by Health Minister Dzulkefly Ahmad while winding up debate on the 2027 budget at committee stage, as reported by Free Malaysia Today and retrieved on 17 November 2026. Every figure in this article comes from that statement. The ministry has not published the contract value, the penalty clauses, the name of the company, or a delivery schedule for the 989.

> **Procurement:** the process by which a government buys goods or services from a private supplier. It runs from tender to contract award to delivery and acceptance. When a supplier fails to deliver, the government's remedy sits in the contract: late-delivery penalties, termination, or a re-tender. Each of those steps takes time, and the ambulance that was never built does not appear while the dispute is resolved.

## Why now

The thread runs back at least to the contract that ended in November 2024. That contract set a target of 590 ambulances by the end of that year. It delivered 115. The failure is therefore roughly two years old, and the ministry is only now putting the number on the record in the House.

The second thread is the budget cycle. Budget 2027 is the vehicle through which the ministry has secured money for 400 more ambulances. Winding-up speeches at committee stage are where ministers answer specific questions from MPs about line items, and the ambulance figure surfaced there because an MP asked about it. That is the mechanism by which a delivery failure becomes a public number: not a press release, but a question in the House.

The third thread is the procurement itself. The ministry describes 989 ambulances as delayed in "current procurement". That phrasing separates the old contract, which is finished, from a new process that has not yet produced vehicles. A re-tender after a failed contract takes time to specify, advertise, evaluate and award. The 989 is the size of the hole, not a delivery order.

## So what

The gain lands on the supplier that eventually wins the new tender. A contract for 989 ambulances, plus 400 funded under Budget 2027, is a large order. Whoever holds it gains years of work. The mechanism is straightforward: a failed contract does not shrink the need, it defers it, and the deferred order is larger than the original.

The cost lands on the patient. An ambulance is not a stock item that sits in a warehouse; it is a vehicle with a crew, a base station and a coverage area. When 475 vehicles that were promised do not arrive, the coverage they were meant to provide does not exist. The household that calls for help waits longer, or travels further, or arranges its own transport. That cost is not on an invoice. It is measured in minutes.

The third actor is the ministry's own procurement machinery. A contract that delivers 19 per cent of its target is a contract that failed at the specification, monitoring or enforcement stage, or at the supplier's capacity. The ministry has named the contractor as the cause. It has not said which of those stages broke, and that distinction decides whether the next contract repeats the failure.

## What it means for Malaysia

The consequence lands on the household in an emergency, and on the district hospital and health clinic that dispatches the vehicle. The concrete carrier is response time: the interval between a call and a crew arriving. Every ambulance that was promised and not delivered is a coverage area that runs on a thinner margin, and the margin is thinnest outside the big cities, where a district hospital covers a wide area with few vehicles.

The second carrier is the public purse. Money allocated for 590 ambulances in the earlier contract did not buy 590 ambulances. Budget 2027 now allocates fresh money for 400 more. A household reading this should understand the sequence: the same need is being funded twice in part, once for vehicles that never arrived and once for vehicles that have not yet been ordered.

The third carrier is the health workforce. The Health Ministry has separately reported that it has filled 11,969 of 18,640 approved posts this year, with 4,253 officers placed — the staffing side of the same system. An ambulance without a crew does not move. Readers following the ministry's capacity story can start with [that staffing picture](/blog/moh-health-posts-11969-filled-2027-placement/).

For readers in flood-prone states, the timing matters. Selangor has mapped 56 flood hotspots and 232 flood-prone areas as the Northeast Monsoon begins, and emergency response is exactly when vehicle availability is tested — see [the Selangor flood hotspot map](/blog/selangor-flood-hotspots-56-northeast-monsoon-2026/). A thinner ambulance fleet is felt hardest in the weeks when roads and demand both change.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The figures come from a single document: the Health Ministry's statement to the Dewan Rakyat, made by Minister Dzulkefly Ahmad during the winding-up of the 2027 budget debate at committee stage, as reported by Free Malaysia Today and retrieved on 17 November 2026. That document states the 590 target, the 115 delivered, the November 2024 contract end, the 989 in current procurement delay, the 400 funded under Budget 2027, and the 1,389 total. The 475 shortfall is arithmetic on two of those figures, not a separate claim by the ministry.

**Inferred.** The link between a missing ambulance and a longer wait for a household is an inference about how emergency coverage works, not a finding in the statement. The ministry did not publish response times, coverage areas or the distribution of the shortfall across states. The inference rests on a named mechanism: fewer vehicles in a coverage area means each remaining vehicle travels further, and travel time is part of response time. The statement also does not say whether the 989 and the 400 overlap or are separate orders; the 1,389 total implies they are counted together, but the ministry's wording does not settle it.

**What would change my mind.** A published contract award showing the supplier's delivery schedule and penalty terms; a ministry breakdown of where the 475 missing vehicles were meant to be based; or a statement that the 989 figure already includes the 400 funded under Budget 2027. Any of those would move the analysis from a gap in the record to a dated plan. Until then, the honest position is that the size of the hole is documented and the timetable for filling it is not.
