## AI Summary

From 1 October 2026, Cai Ku [2026] No. 12 governs Chinese government emergency procurement. Imported goods no longer wait on a provincial finance-bureau approval; the buyer files the record instead. Contracts with a prepayment arrangement are encouraged to pay 40% or more up front. Add-on orders from the same supplier are capped at 30% of the original contract. The award is published within 10 working days, or within 5 working days of the emergency ending. The gain is cash timing, not price.

## What happened

The Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Emergency Management issued 财库〔2026〕12号, the Interim Measures for Emergency Procurement Management, published 14 September 2026. Article 29 sets the effective date at 1 October 2026.

The instrument does four things a foreign supplier can price.

**It replaces approval with a filing.** Emergency procurement under these measures proceeds on the buyer's own record rather than on a prior approval from the provincial finance department. That is the change that matters for imported goods, because the approval step was the one that sat between a confirmed order and a signed contract.

**It pushes prepayment up.** Article 17 states that where a contract carries a prepayment arrangement, the buyer is encouraged to raise the prepayment share to 40% or more of the contract, to keep the emergency procurement moving in an orderly way.

**It caps add-ons.** Article 15 lets the buyer purchase additional goods, works or services with the same subject matter from the original supplier, and states that the total of those add-ons in principle does not exceed 30% of the original contract amount.

**It sets publication and retention clocks.** Article 13 requires the award result to be published on a media outlet designated by the finance department at provincial level or above within 10 working days of the winning supplier being confirmed. Where timely publication is not possible, the outer limit is 5 working days after the emergency ends. Article 18 requires the procurement files to be kept for at least 15 years from the end of the procurement.

Article 11 removes the usual three-supplier floor: where only two suppliers, or only one, submit bids or responses or pass the qualification and compliance review, and the procurement documents carry no unreasonable terms, the procurement continues.

> **Emergency procurement** — a purchase a Chinese government buyer makes under the shortened procedure in Cai Ku [2026] No. 12 when an emergency leaves no time for the ordinary tendering cycle. The buyer records the purchase and files it; it does not wait for a prior approval before signing.

## Why now

The measures took effect on 1 October 2026, and the publication date of 14 September 2026 left suppliers roughly two weeks to read them before they applied. The instrument is joint, issued by the finance ministry together with the emergency management ministry, which tells you the buyer base it is written for: agencies that respond to disasters and supply shocks, not the general run of government purchasing.

The timing also lands inside a quarter when Chinese buyers are already being pushed to pay suppliers faster. The same policy direction shows up in the factory-profit data covered in [China's Factory Profits Rose 15.7%, and Their Customers Started Paying Later](/blog/china-industrial-profits-q4-supply-chain/), where the gap between reported profit and cash collected widened. Emergency procurement is the one channel where the state is now writing faster payment into the rule rather than leaving it to negotiation.

## So what

The judgement is narrow and it is worth money. A supplier selling into this channel gets paid earlier and gets a contract signed without the approval queue. It does not get a higher price, and it does not get relief from any duty, VAT or customs procedure. The 40% is a payment term, not a subsidy.

Work the arithmetic on a contract of CNY 1,000,000, a round figure used only to show the mechanics:

| Item | Ordinary terms | Emergency terms under the measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contract value | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
| Prepayment share | 0 | 400,000 (40%) |
| Balance on delivery | 1,000,000 | 600,000 |
| Working capital the supplier funds until delivery | 1,000,000 | 600,000 |
| Add-on headroom from the same supplier | none stated | 300,000 (30% of original) |

The prepayment line is the one to take to a bank. If the supplier funds production on borrowed money at 6% a year and the contract runs 90 days from signature to delivery, the ordinary terms cost 1,000,000 × 6% × 90/365 = 14,795 in interest. The emergency terms cost 600,000 × 6% × 90/365 = 8,877. The difference is 5,918 on this contract, or 0.59% of contract value. On a CNY 10,000,000 contract the same arithmetic returns 59,178. That is the size of the prize: real, repeatable, and small next to a duty change.

The add-on cap is the second number to hold. A buyer who needs more of the same goods after the first award goes back to the original supplier, and the ceiling on that second order is 30% of the original contract. On the CNY 1,000,000 contract the buyer can add up to CNY 300,000 without running a new procurement. A supplier who knows this prices the first contract with the follow-on in mind, because the follow-on is the cheapest order the buyer will place all year and the supplier who holds the first contract holds the right of first refusal on it.

The publication clock is the third. Ten working days from confirmation to a public award notice means a losing bidder learns the outcome inside two weeks, and a winning supplier can point a bank or a customs broker at a public record of the contract. Where the emergency made timely publication impossible, the notice lands within 5 working days of the emergency ending, so the record exists even for the fastest purchases.

The 15-year retention rule is the one that changes how a supplier should behave. Every document in the file survives longer than most of the people who signed it. A quotation, a specification sheet, a delivery note or a certificate of origin that goes into an emergency procurement file is discoverable for a decade and a half. Suppliers who treat emergency paperwork as disposable are building a liability with a long tail.

For a foreign supplier the practical sequence is unchanged at the border. The goods still clear customs under the ordinary rules, and the documents that decide whether the container moves are the ones set out in [Importing From China: The Documents That Decide Whether the Container Moves](/blog/importing-from-china-documents-checklist/). What changes is on the Chinese side of the contract: the buyer files instead of waiting, and the supplier collects 40% before shipping.

One more case, because the prepayment is the whole story. Take a contract of CNY 4,000,000 with a 120-day production run and the same 6% cost of money. Ordinary terms: 4,000,000 × 6% × 120/365 = 78,904. Emergency terms at 40% prepaid: 2,400,000 × 6% × 120/365 = 47,342. The saving is 31,562, or 0.79% of contract value. The longer the production run, the more the prepayment is worth, because the supplier is funding less of the contract for more days. A supplier quoting into this channel should price the prepayment into the offer rather than treat it as a favour.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The effective date of 1 October 2026, the 40% prepayment encouragement in Article 17, the 30% add-on cap in Article 15, the 10-working-day and 5-working-day publication deadlines in Article 13, the 15-year retention period in Article 18 and the two-or-one-supplier rule in Article 11 all appear in the text of Cai Ku [2026] No. 12 as retrieved on 26 October 2026. The article numbers and the quoted Chinese text are on the page.

**Inferred.** The claim that imported goods no longer need a provincial finance-bureau approval rests on the structure of the instrument rather than on a sentence that names imported goods. The measures set out a filing-based procedure and do not reproduce the prior-approval step; I read that as its removal, and a reader should treat it as my reading of a single document. The interest arithmetic in "So what" uses a 6% cost of money and 90- and 120-day runs that I chose to show the mechanics; they are not in the instrument. The CNY 1,000,000 and CNY 4,000,000 contract values are illustrative. Every figure traces to one source, and no second document was cross-checked.

**What would change my mind.** A provincial finance department notice, or a finance-ministry interpretation, stating that imported goods in emergency procurement still require a prior approval would overturn the central claim. A circular narrowing "encouraged" to a hard obligation, or capping the prepayment below 40%, would change the cash arithmetic. A published award notice showing an imported-goods emergency contract paid on ordinary terms would show the encouragement is not being followed in practice.

## For you

- **Importers holding in-transit cargo:** file every declaration before the deadline, because the border procedure does not change and the emergency contract does not move your container.
- **Suppliers quoting into an emergency tender:** put the 40% prepayment in the offer as a term, not a request, and price the balance on the assumption it arrives after delivery.
- **Suppliers who already hold an emergency contract:** plan for the 30% add-on now, because the follow-on order comes to you and the buyer has no reason to run a new procurement for it.
- **Anyone signing an emergency contract:** keep every quotation, specification and delivery record for the full 15 years, because the file outlives the deal.

## FAQ

**Does an imported product still need a provincial finance-bureau approval for emergency procurement after 1 October 2026?**

The measures set out a filing-based procedure and do not reproduce the prior-approval step that used to gate imported goods. On my reading of Cai Ku [2026] No. 12, the buyer records the emergency procurement and proceeds, rather than waiting for an approval before signing. That reading rests on the structure of a single document, and no supplementary guidance naming imported goods has been published. A supplier should ask the buying agency to confirm in writing which step it is following before committing to a delivery date, because the answer decides whether the contract can be signed this week or next month.

**Is the 40% prepayment a legal entitlement or a target?**

Article 17 uses the word encouraged. It applies where the contract already carries a prepayment arrangement, and it asks the buyer to raise that share to 40% or more. It is not a right a supplier can enforce, and it does not create a prepayment where the contract has none. Treat it as the number to write into your offer and to cite when the buyer proposes a lower figure. A buyer who declines is not breaking the rule; a buyer who accepts is following the policy direction the instrument sets.

**How long does the award stay on the public record?**

The award result is published within 10 working days of the winning supplier being confirmed, or within 5 working days of the emergency ending where timely publication was not possible. The procurement files themselves are kept for at least 15 years from the end of the procurement. That retention period is the one that matters for a supplier, because every quotation and delivery record in the file stays available for a decade and a half. Keep your own copies to match.

## The point

The change is about timing, not price. A supplier selling into Chinese government emergency procurement after 1 October 2026 gets a contract signed without the approval queue and collects 40% before shipping, and that is worth roughly 0.6% to 0.8% of contract value in the cost of money on the runs modelled here. The border is untouched: customs, duty and documents work exactly as they did before. Price the prepayment into the offer, plan for the 30% add-on, and keep the file for 15 years.
