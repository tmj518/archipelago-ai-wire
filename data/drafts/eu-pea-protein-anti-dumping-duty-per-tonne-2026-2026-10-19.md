## AI Summary

The European Commission has imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on pea protein from China of 40.5% to 67.1%, in place for five years from 25 September 2026. The duty is ad valorem, so the landed-cost uplift is that percentage of your customs value: on a consignment valued at 10,000, the duty is 4,050 at the low end and 6,710 at the high end. The cost lands on the EU importer, and the first decision is which rate your supplier attracts.

## What happened

The European Commission has imposed definitive anti-dumping duties on pea protein from China. The band runs from **40.5% to 67.1%** ad valorem, and the duties are in place for **five years** from 25 September 2026.

> "The definitive anti-dumping duties imposed range from 40.5% to 67.1% and will be in place for five years."
> — European Commission, DG Trade and Economic Security, "Commission acts against imports of pea protein from China", 25 September 2026

Those are the only two rates the Commission's announcement gives. It does not publish, in the text this article is built from, which exporter sits at which end of the band, and it does not give a per-tonne figure. So this article does not invent one. What it can do is show you the arithmetic you need to run on your own invoice, because an ad valorem duty is a percentage of a value you already know.

## Why now

The definitive duties took effect on 25 September 2026. They were not the first measure: provisional duties had been in force since **29 April 2026**. If you have imported since late April, you have already been paying at the provisional rate, and the definitive decision is what fixes the position for the five years that follow.

That matters for two reasons. First, any consignment entered during the provisional period was assessed under the provisional regime, not this one — check your entries rather than assuming the definitive rate applies retroactively to everything. Second, the five-year clock started on 25 September 2026, so the window in which you are choosing between absorbing, re-pricing and re-sourcing is a long one, but it is a window with a known end.

## So what

A duty band is not a single cost. It is a range, and the gap between the ends is wide: 67.1% is roughly 1.66 times 40.5%. Two importers bringing in the same product, at the same customs value, can face duty bills that differ by more than half again depending on which rate their supplier attracts.

That makes the first question for any importer not "how much is the duty" but "which rate applies to my supplier". The Commission's announcement sets the band; the rate that binds you is a matter of your exporter's position in the case, and you should get it in writing rather than assume the low end.

The Commission puts the EU market for pea protein at some **€175m**. That is the size of the pool the duty is being applied to, and it is worth holding in mind for a reason that has nothing to do with your own margin: a duty of this size on a market of that size is a change the whole supply chain will be reacting to at once. Buyers who can move will be looking at the same alternatives you are.

## How confident

**Confirmed.** The rates of 40.5% and 67.1%, the five-year duration from 25 September 2026, the provisional start date of 29 April 2026 and the €175m market value all come from the Commission's own announcement of 25 September 2026, and the operative sentence is quoted in full above.

**Inferred.** The per-unit duty figures are arithmetic, not measurement. The announcement states the duty as a percentage of value and gives no customs value, no volume and no per-tonne figure, so every figure in the table below is the stated rate applied to a customs value this article chose for illustration. The method is exact for whatever value you put into it; the illustration is not a statement about what anyone else is paying.

**What would change this.** A published per-exporter rate table would replace the band with a single number for your supplier and make the low-end assumption testable. A customs value materially different from the illustrations would move every duty figure proportionally. And if the Commission's announcement is later amended on duration or scope, the five-year window and the product coverage both change.

## For you

- **Importers already bringing in Chinese pea protein:** get your supplier's applicable rate in writing before the next entry, then multiply your customs value by it — 40.5% at the low end, 67.1% at the high end — and decide whether to absorb or re-price.
- **Buyers still on provisional-period entries:** check what was assessed between 29 April 2026 and 25 September 2026 rather than assuming the definitive rate applies to those consignments.
- **Importers whose margin cannot carry the upper rate:** price the alternative supply now, while the five-year window is still long, and compare it against the duty-inclusive landed cost rather than against the pre-duty price.
- **Forwarders and brokers quoting landed cost:** the duty is charged on customs value, so a higher freight or insurance figure that feeds into that value raises the duty too — quote it as a separate line.

## FAQ

**What rate of duty applies to my pea protein from China?**
The Commission's announcement sets a band of 40.5% to 67.1% and does not say, in the text this article is built from, which exporter attracts which rate. So the answer for your consignment is not in the announcement: it depends on your supplier's position in the case. Ask your supplier or your customs broker for the rate that applies to your exporter, and get it in writing before you price the next order. Do not assume the 40.5% floor applies to you simply because it is the lower number.

**How much does the duty add to a consignment?**
It adds the stated percentage of your customs value. On a customs value of 10,000 the duty is 4,050 at 40.5% and 6,710 at 67.1%. On 50,000 it is 20,250 and 33,550. The duty is ad valorem, so it scales with value, not with weight or volume: a heavier consignment at the same value carries the same duty. Add the result to your existing freight, insurance and other charges to get the landed-cost uplift from this measure alone.

**Do the duties apply to imports I made earlier this year?**
The definitive duties took effect on 25 September 2026 and run for five years. Before that, provisional duties had been in force since 29 April 2026. Consignments entered during the provisional period were assessed under the provisional regime, so the definitive rate is not automatically the rate that applied to them. Check your entries for the period from 29 April 2026 rather than assuming either that the definitive rate applies retroactively or that nothing was payable before late September.

**Is it cheaper to re-source than to pay the duty?**
That depends on a figure this article does not have: the price gap between Chinese pea protein and the alternatives. The duty is a known percentage of customs value, so you can compute the duty-inclusive landed cost exactly. What you cannot compute from the Commission's announcement is what an alternative supplier would charge. Compare the two directly — duty-inclusive Chinese cost against alternative cost — rather than assuming a duty of this size closes the gap. It does, or it does not, and the comparison is the only way to know.

## How we calculated this

The duty is ad valorem, so the calculation is short. Take the customs value of the consignment — the figure your entry is assessed on — and multiply it by the rate that applies to your supplier.

```
duty = customs value x rate

lower bound:  4,050 = 10,000 x 0.405
upper bound:  6,710 = 10,000 x 0.671
gap:          2,660 = 6,710 - 4,050

at 50,000:   20,250 = 50,000 x 0.405
             33,550 = 50,000 x 0.671
```

Worked on a customs value of 100 units of currency, the duty is 40.5 at the low end and 67.1 at the high end. Scale that to your own consignment: a customs value of 10,000 becomes 4,050 of duty at 40.5%, or 6,710 at 67.1%. The difference between the two ends on that consignment is 2,660 — the amount at stake in getting the rate right.

This is the whole of the landed-cost uplift from the duty itself. It sits on top of whatever you already pay in freight, insurance and any other charges, and it is charged on value, not on weight, so a heavier or lighter consignment at the same value carries the same duty.

## The point

The duty is not a mystery number to be discovered; it is a percentage you can apply today. What is genuinely unknown is which end of the 40.5% to 67.1% band your supplier sits at, and that is a question for your paperwork, not for the Commission's press release. Get the rate, multiply your customs value, and the five-year decision — absorb, re-price or re-source — becomes arithmetic instead of guesswork.
