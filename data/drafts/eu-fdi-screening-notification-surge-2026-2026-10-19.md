## AI Summary

The European Commission's sixth annual FDI screening report records 667 investments notified in 2025, up from 477 in 2024 — a 40% increase. 88% of those cases closed within 15 days. 12% went to an in-depth security risk assessment, and 53% of the in-depth cases were in manufacturing. The new Foreign Investment Screening Regulation applies from 17 January 2028. For an importer buying a Chinese-owned plant or building one in the EU, the cost is the delay on the one deal in eight that does not clear in 15 days.

## What happened

The European Commission published its sixth annual report on the screening of foreign direct investment into the EU. The headline movement is in the volume of deals that reached the EU cooperation mechanism, the channel through which Member States notify the Commission of investments that pass their national screens.

In 2025, Member States notified **667** investments to the Commission. In 2024 the figure was **477**. That is a **40%** increase between the two years.

The outcome split is the part that matters to anyone planning a deal:

| Outcome | Share of the 667 notified cases, 2025 |
| --- | --- |
| Closed within 15 days | 88% |
| Required an in-depth security risk assessment | 12% |
| In-depth cases in manufacturing | 53% of in-depth cases |

The 12% figure is described in the report as a 4% rise compared with the previous year. The manufacturing share of in-depth cases, 53%, is up from 50% in 2024.

Separately, the EU's new Foreign Investment Screening Regulation (FISR) was adopted on 17 June 2026 and applies from **17 January 2028**. The report covers the year before that regulation takes effect; the 2025 numbers describe the regime as it runs now, not the one that starts in 2028.

> **Cooperation mechanism** — the EU process under which a Member State that screens an investment notifies the European Commission and the other Member States, who then have a window to comment or raise concerns. A deal "notified" under this mechanism has already entered a national screen; the notification is not the start of screening, it is the point at which the deal becomes visible to the rest of the EU.

## Why now

The report lands in October 2026, four months after the FISR was adopted on 17 June 2026 and fifteen months before it applies on 17 January 2028. That gap is the reason to read the 2025 numbers now rather than in 2028.

The 2025 figures describe behaviour under the current framework. The FISR replaces that framework from 17 January 2028. An investor who reads only the 2025 report is reading the last full year of the old regime; an investor who waits for the 2028 report to size the risk has already committed capital.

The volume move is the signal. Notifications rose 40% in a single year, from 477 to 667. The in-depth share rose to 12%, a 4% increase on the prior year. Both moved in the same direction at the same time.

## So what

The judgement is this: the base case for a Chinese acquisition or greenfield investment in the EU is a 15-day close, and the tail case is an in-depth assessment. Price the tail, not the base.

Start with the arithmetic on the 667 notified cases. 88% closed within 15 days. 12% did not. On 667 cases, 12% is roughly 80 deals that went to in-depth review in 2025. That is the population that carries the delay.

Now apply it to a single deal. An importer acquiring a Chinese-owned manufacturing plant in the EU is buying into the segment where the in-depth share concentrates: 53% of in-depth cases were in manufacturing. A manufacturing deal is not drawing from the 12% average; it is drawing from a pool where manufacturing is the majority of the in-depth caseload.

Work one case. A Chinese-owned components plant in the EU is on the block at a purchase price the buyer has already modelled. The buyer's closing timetable assumes the 15-day path — the 88% outcome. If the deal lands in the 12%, the 15-day assumption is wrong and the closing date moves to whenever the in-depth assessment concludes. The report does not give a duration for in-depth assessments, so the buyer cannot put a number of days on the tail. What the buyer can do is hold the deal's financing and integration costs against a closing date that is not 15 days out.

Work a second case, the greenfield route. A Chinese manufacturer building a new EU plant is not acquiring an existing entity, but the investment still enters a national screen and, if notified, the cooperation mechanism. The 667 figure counts notified investments, and the report does not split acquisitions from greenfield in the figures supplied here. The exposure is the same shape: the deal either clears in 15 days or it does not, and the 12% is the share that does not.

Work a third case, the portfolio route. An importer that holds several small EU investments rather than one large one faces the 12% once per notified deal, not once per group. At 667 notifications and 12% in-depth, the per-deal probability is the unit that matters. A group with four notifiable deals is running the 12% four times.

The cost is not a duty and it is not a tariff. It is the cost of capital and the cost of a plan built on a closing date that holds 88% of the time. The number to carry into the deal model is 12%, and the segment to carry alongside it is manufacturing at 53% of in-depth cases.

The 40% move is the largest single-year change the supplied figures record, and it is the number to use when sizing how much busier the mechanism has become.
